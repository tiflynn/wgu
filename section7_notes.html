01 · Why Financial Statement Analysis?

Topic 6.1

  • Purpose: diagnose existing problems and forecast future performance — predict future profitability & cash flows from the past, and evaluate performance to identify problem areas.
  • Two dimensions of comparison: across time for the same company (trend), and across companies at the same point in time (benchmark).
  • Key insight: a ratio alone means very little — it becomes informative only when compared against past values and industry peers.
  • Two common methods: Ratios (relationships between financial statement numbers) and Common-Size Financial Statements (a special category of ratio — everything divided by sales).

02 · The 6 Widely Used Ratios

Topic 6.2 — memorize these six cold

Debt Ratio Leverage
Total LiabilitiesTotal Assets
Means: % of assets financed by borrowing vs. shareholder money.
Normal range: 50%–60%
Return on Sales (ROS) Profitability
Net IncomeSales
Means: pennies of profit kept from each dollar of sales. Also called "margin."
No single normal range — varies widely by industry (e.g., Walmart ~1–2%, a jewelry store much higher). Judge against industry peers, not a fixed benchmark.
Return on Equity (ROE) Profitability
Net IncomeStockholders' Equity
Means: pennies earned during the year for each dollar shareholders invested.
No fixed normal range given in the notes — compare to the company's own historical trend and industry peers. Watch for ROE inflated mainly by leverage rather than genuine profitability/efficiency.
Current Ratio Liquidity
Current AssetsCurrent Liabilities
Means: ability to pay short-term (within one year) bills using short-term assets.
No specific range given in the notes — general rule of thumb is above 1.0 to cover short-term obligations; the specific comfortable level varies by industry.
Asset Turnover Efficiency
SalesTotal Assets
Means: dollars of sales generated by each dollar of assets — how hard assets are working.
No single normal range — depends heavily on business model (high for Walmart-style volume businesses, low for jewelry-store-style margin businesses). Best read alongside Return on Sales.
Price-Earnings (P/E) Ratio Market Valuation
Market Value of SharesNet Income
Means: $ investors pay for $1 of earnings. Growth potential, earnings stability & management quality, priced in. Equivalent per-share version: Price per Share / EPS (share counts cancel).
No fixed normal range given in the notes — higher signals stronger growth expectations (or overvaluation); lower signals weaker growth expectations (or undervaluation). Interpreted relative to peers and the market, not an absolute number.
Note: P/E is the only one of the six that's market-based — it pulls in stock price, not just financial-statement numbers. The other five live entirely inside the balance sheet / income statement.
On "normal ranges": Debt Ratio (50%–60%) is the only ratio in this set of six with an explicit numeric benchmark from the course materials. The rest are context-dependent — always interpreted against a company's own trend over time and against industry peers, not a single fixed number. Don't force-memorize a range that doesn't exist.

03 · Common-Size Financial Statements

Topic 6.3

Shows all amounts for a given year as a percentage of a chosen denominator. Especially useful for comparing companies of very different sizes.

StatementDenominatorReveals
Income StatementAlways SalesPennies of each expense per $1 of sales — best tool for pinpointing a profitability problem
Balance Sheet ÷ SalesSalesEfficiency — pennies of each asset needed to generate $1 of sales
Balance Sheet ÷ AssetsTotal AssetsAsset Mix — % of total assets held in each category
Exam-ready: Common-size statements are the fastest, most intuitive starting point for any financial statement analysis — reach for these first.

04 · The DuPont Framework

Topic 6.4 — decomposes ROE into 3 drivers

ROE (full decomposition)
Return on Sales × Asset Turnover × Assets-to-Equity
= Net Income / Stockholders' Equity (the Sales and Total Assets terms cancel algebraically)
ComponentFormulaMeasures
ProfitabilityNet IncomeSalesReturn on Sales
EfficiencySalesTotal AssetsAsset Turnover
LeverageTotal AssetsEquityAssets-to-Equity (Equity Multiplier)
Exam trap: A high ROE isn't automatically good — if it's driven mainly by leverage, that's more fixed interest obligations regardless of how business is going, i.e., more financial risk.

Big idea: No single "correct" combination — margin and turnover are a trade-off. Different business models land at different points on the spectrum while still being profitable.

The leverage chain — how borrowing (using other people's money) boosts ROE without shareholders contributing more equity.

RatioFormulaResult FormatNormal Range

Worked example (Liabilities = $1, Equity = $1, Assets = $2):
Debt Ratio = $1/$2 = 50%  |  Debt-to-Equity = $1/$1 = 1.0  |  Assets-to-Equity = $2/$1 = 2.0
Same financing structure — different lens. Like Celsius vs. Fahrenheit vs. Kelvin: same temperature, different-looking numbers.

Why Walmart/Target run above "normal": collateral quality. Land and buildings are strong, seizable collateral — banks lend more comfortably against tangible assets than against spending on salaries/marketing (which leaves nothing to repossess).

07 · Times Interest Earned

The "banker's ratio" — a flow measure, not a balance-sheet snapshot

Times Interest Earned (TIE)
EBITInterest Expense
EBIT = Earnings Before Interest & Taxes = Operating Income

The "pie" — operating income gets split in strict legal priority order:

1st
Lenders
Interest expense — legal priority
2nd
Government
Taxes, on what's left
3rd
Shareholders
Net income, on what's left
The "Add-Back" — getting to EBIT
EBIT = Net Income + Tax Expense + Interest Expense
Net Income is already after interest & taxes were subtracted — reverse that to isolate EBIT.
TIE ResultInterpretation
9.3× (Walmart)Large safety cushion — lenders confident
1.0×Barely covers interest — nothing left for taxes/shareholders. Any disruption = missed payment.

08 · Cash Flow Ratios

Based on the statement of cash flows, not the balance sheet or income statement

When these matter most: (1) large non-cash expenses distorting net income, (2) rapid growth burning cash while net income looks strong, (3) "window dressing" before a loan/IPO — cash is harder to manipulate than net income.
Cash Flow to Net Income Ratio Cash Flow
Operating Cash FlowNet Income
Means: how much accrual assumptions are influencing net income. Usually > 1.0 — mainly due to depreciation (reduces Net Income, but isn't a cash outflow). A big year-over-year swing = investigate (e.g., via common-size income statement).
Cash Flow Adequacy Ratio Cash Flow
Cash from OperationsCash for Investing Activities
Means: can the company fund capex + acquisitions using only operating cash? >1.0 = self-sufficient, with options (pay down debt, dividends, reinvest). Investing = capex + acquisitions only — not dividends (financing) or interest (operating).
Cash Times Interest Earned Cash Flow
Operating Cash Flow Before Interest & TaxesCash Paid for Interest
Means: the cash-based version of TIE — strips out accrual assumptions entirely. Get worried as this approaches 1.0, same as regular TIE.

09 · Potential Pitfalls of Financial Statement Analysis

Topic 6.6 — four traps to watch for

1

Incomplete Information

Statements look precise (exact dollars) but contain estimates. Qualitative business judgment can matter just as much, or more.

2

Lack of Comparability

Classification (Nordstrom bundles rent into COGS), conglomerates (no clean peer for GE), accounting methods (different depreciation assumptions on identical equipment).

3

The "Smoking Gun" Fallacy

Rarely one single cause behind a result — usually many small contributing factors. (Business-school cases are curated to have one clean cause; real companies aren't.)

4

Anchoring & Timeliness

Long historical trends can overshadow recent, timely information. What happened last week can matter more than a 10-year trend.

10 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The mix-ups that show up most often

  • Debt Ratio vs. Debt-to-Equity vs. Assets-to-Equity — always check the denominator first. ÷ Assets = percentage. ÷ Equity = multiple.
  • P/E Ratio — Market Value of Shares / Net Income (whole-company) is the same ratio as Price/Share ÷ EPS (per-share) — share counts cancel.
  • Cash Flow Adequacy — "investing activities" = capex + acquisitions ONLY. Dividends = financing. Interest = operating. Both are common distractors.
  • Times Interest Earned — if given Net Income, you MUST add back both Tax Expense and Interest Expense to reach EBIT before dividing.
  • The "Big 6" ratios are Debt Ratio, ROS, ROE, Current Ratio, Asset Turnover, P/E. Cash flow ratios (Adequacy, Cash TIE, Cash Flow to Net Income) are a separate family — not part of the Big 6.
  • Common-size denominator — Income Statement is always Sales. Balance Sheet can be Sales (→ efficiency) OR Total Assets (→ asset mix).
  • "All of these are correct" answer choices — when every specific listed item is a legitimate example of the concept being tested, the "all of the above"-style option is often the correct one, not a trap.
  • Watch for distractor data — practice problems often include extra given numbers (Total Assets, Financing Cash Flow, etc.) that don't belong in the formula being asked about. Identify the correct formula first, then pull only the numbers it needs.
