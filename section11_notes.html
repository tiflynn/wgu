01 · Why ABC Exists

Topic 10.1

Activity-Based Costing (ABC)
Assign overhead based on the ACTIVITIES that actually create it
ABC is a method of attributing overhead costs to products based on measurable factors that relate to the activities that create those overhead costs — a deliberate alternative to spreading one flat rate over everything.

Direct materials and direct labor are easy to trace to a specific product. Manufacturing overhead is the hard one — electricity, supervisor salaries, machine depreciation don't obviously belong to any one product. The traditional fix is to divide total overhead by total units produced and apply that single rate to everything.

Lily's Ice Cream Company: for years Lily made only Vanilla, so total overhead ÷ total gallons worked fine — every gallon really was roughly identical. Once she added five gourmet flavors (each needing a flavor chemist, more inspectors, more setups, more accounting), the flat rate broke down: Vanilla suddenly looked unprofitable, even though it's the simplest, cheapest-to-make flavor she has.
The hidden assumption in a flat overhead rate: a single traditional rate assumes every product's production process creates the same amount of overhead. That's fine with one product — false the moment products start to differ in complexity, batch size, or number of ingredients.

  • Identify overhead cost activities — what does the business actually do that creates overhead? (e.g., running machines, switching batches, servicing a flavor's special needs, keeping the facility open)
  • Analyze individual overhead costs in terms of those activities — go line item by line item and figure out which activity (or activities) is really causing each cost. Some are easy (electricity is 100% routine production); some require real investigation (time logs, surveys) since one department's cost can split across several activities.
  • Identify measurable cost drivers — a numerical stand-in for each activity (gallons, batches, ingredients).
  • Assign overhead — turn cost pools and drivers into a rate, then apply that rate to each product.
  • Use the ABC data to make decisions — set prices, spot unprofitable products, fix expensive production processes.
REMEMBER THIS (verbatim):
  • Companies that better understand their costs have an advantage over their competitors.
  • When using an ABC system, a company identifies specific business activities that create overhead costs and then assigns overhead to products or divisions based on the level of those activities.
  • The ultimate purpose of an ABC system is to help a company make better decisions.

03 · The Four ABC Cost Categories

Topic 10.2

CategoryTriggered byTypical driverExamples
Unit-level Producing one more unit Units / gallons produced Machine maintenance, machine depreciation, electricity & other energy costs
Batch-level Starting/stopping a production run, regardless of its size Number of batches Inspections, machine setups, movement of & accounting for materials
Product line Supporting one specific product line, regardless of batches or units produced Number of ingredients (or similar complexity measure) Engineering product design, special warehouse storage, a dedicated product-line supervisor, ordering/purchasing unique materials
Facility support Simply having a facility, unrelated to any specific product No driver — not assigned Property taxes, factory insurance, security, landscaping, general accounting, general factory administration
1

Unit-Level

Scales directly with volume — one more unit means one more round of routine, traditional overhead.

2

Batch-Level

Triggered by the changeover itself — a huge batch and a tiny batch each cost the same amount once.

3

Product Line

Exists because a product/flavor exists at all — independent of how many units or batches get made.

4

Facility Support

The cost of existing as a company. Doesn't care what — or whether anything — gets produced.

Cost pool: the total dollar amount of overhead grouped together because it's caused by the same activity. Step 2 (analyzing line items) is what produces these four pools — e.g., Lily's $1,740,000 in total overhead split into $750,000 (unit-level) + $440,000 (batch-level) + $270,000 (product line) + $280,000 (facility support).
REMEMBER THIS: in an ABC system, facility support overhead costs are treated as common costs that are not assigned to any specific product, division, or product line. Nothing about production activity moves this cost, so there's no valid basis to spread it — it's set aside, not eliminated. It still has to get paid for out of the profits the products actually generate.

04 · Cost Drivers

Topic 10.3

Cost Driver REMEMBER THIS — verbatim
A NUMERICAL measure, not a description
A cost driver is a numerical measure used to reflect the amount of a specific overhead cost that is associated with a particular activity. "This flavor is messier" isn't a cost driver — "number of ingredients" is.
Worker turnover example: if Division A has 15 turnover events in a year and Division B has only 3, Division A should be assigned more of the overhead tied to turnover (interviewing, documentation, training) — because number of turnover events is a countable, measurable driver of that cost.
What makes a driver "good": it has to be quantifiable and have a real cause-and-effect relationship with the activity. If you can't count it, it can't drive the cost — that's why "number of ingredients" stands in for recipe complexity, and "number of batches" stands in for changeover activity.

05 · Assigning Overhead to Production

Topic 10.4

The textbook breaks Step 4 into three necessary sub-steps:

1st
Gather the data
Overhead cost & cost driver counts
2nd
Compute the rate
Per cost driver event
3rd
Apply the rate
To assign overhead to production
Overhead Rate per Cost Driver Event
Cost Pool ÷ Total Cost Driver Events
Example (Lily's unit-level pool): $750,000 ÷ 1,500,000 gallons = $0.50/gallon. Same logic gives $440,000 ÷ 500 batches = $880/batch, and $270,000 ÷ 100 ingredients = $2,700/ingredient. Always divide by the combined total driver events across every product — not just one product's share.
Overhead Assigned to a Specific Product
Rate × That Product's Driver Usage — summed across every applicable category
Example (Vanilla, 500,000 gallons / 25 batches / 5 ingredients): (500,000 × $0.50) + (25 × $880) + (5 × $2,700) = $250,000 + $22,000 + $13,500 = $285,500 total ABC overhead.
Reconciliation check: whatever gets assigned to individual products, plus whatever's left unassigned in facility support, always has to add back up to total overhead. If the flavor-by-flavor totals don't match the grand total, the difference is facility support sitting off to the side — not a calculation error.

06 · Using ABC Data to Make Decisions

Topic 10.5

High-Volume, Standard ProductLow-Volume, Unique Product
Batch sizeLarge — few changeoversSmall — frequent changeovers
Recipe/complexitySimple, few ingredientsComplex, many ingredients
Overhead per unitLowestHighest
Under a flat traditional rate...Looks less profitable than it really isLooks more profitable than it really is
General principle: standard, high-volume products create less overhead than a flat rate assumes (large batches + simple recipes), so ABC usually reveals them to be more profitable than they first appeared. Complex, low-volume products create a disproportionate share of overhead (small batches + many ingredients), so ABC usually reveals them to be less profitable than they first appeared.
Two ways to fix an expensive activity, once you know its rate: either charge more (pass the cost of the activity on to whoever is driving it — e.g., a customer demanding small daily batches) or change the process (reduce how often the expensive activity happens — e.g., switch to weekly batches instead of daily). Both are only visible once the ABC rate makes the true cost of the activity obvious.
A

Reflects Economic Reality

The numbers have to be true — accurately showing which products really are profitable and which aren't.

B

Motivates Correct Behavior

The numbers have to point toward action — showing exactly what to change to fix a problem.

REMEMBER THIS (verbatim): these are the two characteristics of a good management accounting measure.

REMEMBER THIS: designing and operating an ABC system usually costs more than a simple traditional system based on a single activity measure (like direct labor hours). That increased cost pays for itself when the improved data leads to significantly better decisions — ABC isn't automatically worth it, it has to earn its keep.

07 · Worked Example — Dakota Company

Applied practice, outside the ice cream story

Step 1 — Compute the rates
Batch-level: $600,000 ÷ (20 + 60) = $7,500/batch  |  Product-line: $400,000 ÷ (10 + 40) = $8,000/line
Add both products' driver counts together before dividing (80 total batches, 50 total lines) — dividing by only one product's count is the most common mistake here.
Step 2 — Assign overhead to Product 1 (20 batches, 10 lines)
(20 × $7,500) + (10 × $8,000) = $150,000 + $80,000 = $230,000
Same two-part-sum pattern as the ice cream flavors — just fewer categories (no unit-level or facility support pool in this problem).
Step 3 — Compute the ABC gross margin
Sales $1,400,000 − DM $200,000 − DL $600,000 − OH $230,000 = $370,000
Product 1's ABC overhead ($230,000) replaces whatever the old single-rate allocation had assigned — the same story as Vanilla, just with generic products instead of ice cream flavors.

08 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The mix-ups that show up most often

  • Total profit and total overhead never change under ABC — only the allocation across products changes. If a problem's grand totals differ between "traditional" and "ABC," something's been miscalculated.
  • Facility support gets NO cost driver — it's a common cost, set aside and not spread on any basis, arbitrary or otherwise. "Not assigned" does not mean "doesn't have to be paid for."
  • Divide by the COMBINED total driver events, not one product's count, when computing a rate (Dakota: 80 total batches, not 20).
  • A cost driver must be numerical — "messy," "complex," or "special" are not cost drivers on their own; they need a countable stand-in (ingredients, batches, setups).
  • Low-volume + unique = highest overhead per unit. High-volume + standard = lowest. Both effects (batch size and complexity) stack in the same direction.
  • Gathering Step 2 data is genuinely hard — time logs, surveys, and employee pushback are real costs of doing ABC well, not just an accounting formality.
  • The five ABC steps, in order: Identify activities → Analyze costs → Identify drivers → Assign overhead → Use the data to decide.
  • ABC costs more to run than a traditional system — it only "pays for itself" when the better data actually changes a decision for the better.
