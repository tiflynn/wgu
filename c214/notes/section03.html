01 · The Big Picture & the Cash Identity

Topic 3.2

Each of the three statements answers a different question. The statement of cash flows (SCF) is the one that follows the actual money.

StatementAnalogyWhat it shows
Income Statement Video camera Results of operations over a period
Balance Sheet Snapshot Assets and financing at one point in time
Statement of Cash Flows Cash tracker All cash moving in and out during a period
The line that defines this whole topic: Cash flow is a fact; net income is an accounting opinion. The SCF is often called the "most honest" statement because it reports cash, not accruals.

Three buckets, three kinds of management decision

Every cash movement is sorted into exactly one of three categories, and each category corresponds to a type of decision management makes.

BucketDecisions it capturesTypical items
CFO
Operating		 What to make, how to make it, for whom, which suppliers to use Cash from customers; payments to suppliers, employees, and tax authorities
CFI
Investing		 Buying and selling long-term assets Purchases of equipment and factories (PP&E)
CFF
Financing		 How the firm pays for its assets Borrowing, repaying debt, issuing or repurchasing stock, dividends
The master identity — use it as a check on every problem
CFO + CFI + CFF = Δ Cash = Ending Cash − Beginning Cash
If your three buckets don't add up to the change in the balance sheet cash account, something is misclassified or has the wrong sign. There is no partial credit on the tie-out — it either reconciles or you have an error.

The standard layout — the order matters: operating, then investing, then financing:

02 · The "Myth" — Cash Flow Can Be Misstated

Topic 3.2

Myth vs. reality
Total cash flow is hard to misstate  |  the split is flexible
The myth: cash flow can't be manipulated, so the SCF can be taken at face value. The reality: the gross (total) cash flow is indeed hard to misstate — but the division between CFO, CFI, and CFF is flexible, so a firm can make one category look better by shrinking another.

Three features of the firm's operating environment drive how cash flows get classified:

1

Core Activities

The same purchase lands in different buckets depending on the business. A hospital bed is a long-term asset for a health care provider (CFI) but inventory for a medical equipment dealer (CFO).

2

Cash Flow Management

Managers whose bonuses depend on CFO are tempted to recategorize items to inflate it. Doing this for a year or two is fairly easy without breaking GAAP.

3

Market Pressure

A firm trying to raise capital does not want to report falling CFO, so managers may use accounting discretion to prop it up.

Takeaway: read the SCF as skeptically as the other two statements. Check the categories, not just the total. A healthy-looking bottom line can hide an operating business that consumes cash.

03 · Why CFO Is Not Net Income

Topic 3.3

CFO covers the cash tied to producing and selling the product — cash in from customers, cash out for materials, operating expenses, and taxes. It looks like net income but isn't, because net income is built on accrual accounting.

Source of differenceWhy it matters
Accounts Receivable (AR) Revenue is recorded when earned, not when collected. If AR rises, the firm booked revenue it has not collected yet. Revenue ≠ cash collected.
Gain or loss on sale of PP&E Gain = sale price − book value. It sits in net income, but the sale proceeds are an investing cash flow (CFI) — so it is not part of CFO.
Non-cash expenses (depreciation) Depreciation reduces net income but no cash leaves the firm. It must be added back.

Two kinds of depreciation

Accounting depreciationEconomic depreciation
What it is A scheduled, mechanical allocation of an asset's cost over its useful life (e.g. straight-line) The actual loss in market value from wear, obsolescence, and falling demand
Where it appears On the income statement Nowhere — it does not directly appear in the statements
Scheduled? Yes No
Cash outlay? No No
Memory tip: neither kind of depreciation is cash. Accounting depreciation is a bookkeeping schedule; economic depreciation is reality. They rarely match, and neither one writes a check.

04 · Calculating CFO — Indirect Method

Topics 3.4 & 3.5

The indirect method is the bottom-up approach used by most analysts: start with net income, then adjust for everything that makes it differ from cash.

 Net Income
+Depreciation (and other non-cash expenses)
−Increase in operating assets (or + a decrease)
+Increase in operating liabilities (or − a decrease)
=CFO
The same thing, in formula-sheet form
CFO = Net Income + Depreciation Expense − Δ Operating Accounts
Δ Operating Accounts = Δ Operating Assets − Δ Operating Liabilities. Note the single minus sign in front of the whole term — that is what produces the opposite signs for assets and liabilities.
Course simplification: assume the firm sells no PP&E, so there is never a gain/loss adjustment to make. If depreciation isn't listed on the income statement, infer it — under the no-disposal assumption, depreciation expense = Δ accumulated depreciation. Other non-cash items (amortization, depletion) are treated exactly like depreciation.

Which accounts are "operating"?

CategoryDefinitionExamples
Operating assets Current assets other than cash Accounts receivable, inventory
Operating liabilities Current liabilities other than notes payable Accounts payable, accrued wages/expenses
Excluded — Cash Its change is the end result of the whole SCF, not an input —
Excluded — Notes payable Even though it sits under current liabilities, interest-bearing borrowing (short- or long-term) is a financing item Goes in CFF

Sign rules — memorize this table

Account typeIf it increasesIf it decreases
Operating asset
AR, inventory		 Cash outflow (−) Cash inflow (+)
Operating liability
AP, accruals		 Cash inflow (+) Cash outflow (−)
Remember: Assets up → cash down. Liabilities up → cash up.
Intuition: buying a car raises your assets and lowers your cash. A rising accounts payable balance means you paid suppliers less cash than your expenses imply — so you kept more cash.

05 · Cash Flow from Investing (CFI)

Topic 3.6

CFI is cash moving in or out because of buying or disposing of fixed assets. Course simplification: no asset sales, and all investing shows up in PP&E.

Two equivalent formulas — pick the one matching the data you're given
CFI = −(Δ Gross PP&E)
or, if only net PP&E is given:
CFI = −(Δ Net PP&E + Depreciation Expense)
  • Gross PP&E — the balance before subtracting accumulated depreciation.
  • Net PP&E — Gross PP&E − Accumulated Depreciation.
  • An increase in PP&E means the firm spent cash on assets, so CFI is an outflow (negative) for a growing firm.
  • Why add depreciation back when starting from net PP&E? Depreciation shrinks net PP&E every year all on its own. So new purchases = (Δ net PP&E) + depreciation. Skip the add-back and you understate what was actually spent.

Mini-example

Net PP&E rises from 800 to 950 and depreciation expense is 120.

 Purchases = (950 − 800) + 120 = 270
=CFI = −270
 Cross-check with gross: accumulated depreciation 200 → 320, so gross PP&E goes 1,000 → 1,270 (Δ = 270) ✓
The sign is the trap. The formulas already carry the negative sign. Compute the change in PP&E, then flip it. A growing firm reports negative CFI — that is normal and healthy, not a red flag.

06 · Cash Flow from Financing & Finding Dividends

Topic 3.7

CFF captures how the firm funds itself: new borrowing, debt repayment, stock issuance, stock repurchase, and dividends.

CFF
CFF = Δ Debt + Δ Stock − Dividends Paid
Δ Debt = notes payable + long-term debt. Δ Stock = common stock + paid-in capital. A financing account that increases is a cash inflow; one that decreases is a cash outflow (repaying lenders or repurchasing stock). Dividends paid are always an outflow.
Formula-sheet note: the C214 reference sheet writes CFF as Δ LT debt + Δ stock − dividends. If the balance sheet includes notes payable, the text treats it as financing too — so include its change.

Finding dividends — they aren't on any statement

Dividends appear on neither the income statement nor the balance sheet. But net income can only be paid out or retained, so it can always be derived:

Step 1 — where net income goes
Net Income = Dividends + Δ Retained Earnings
Step 2 — the retained earnings roll-forward
New RE = Old RE + Net Income − Dividends
Step 3 — rearranged for the piece you actually need
Dividends = (Old RE + Net Income) − New RE
This is the same bridge equation from Topic 2, solved for dividends.
Quick check: if retained earnings rose by less than net income, the difference was paid out as dividends. If RE rose by exactly net income, dividends were zero.

07 · Worked Example — Cedar Ridge Co.

A full SCF from two balance sheets

Given: net income = 300. No assets were sold. Dividends are not stated, so they have to be derived from retained earnings. Beginning cash was 200.

Inventory300280−20+20CFO

Step 1 — depreciation (not given, so infer it)

Δ Accumulated Depreciation = 560 − 500 = 60

Step 2 — dividends (not given either)

(370 + 300) − 550 = 120
Net income was 300 but retained earnings only rose 180, so 120 went out the door.

Step 3 — CFO

 300 net income
+60 depreciation add-back
−50 AR rose (asset up, cash down)
+20 inventory fell (asset down, cash up)
+60 AP rose (liability up, cash up)
−10 accruals fell (liability down, cash down)
=CFO = 380

Step 4 — CFI

CFI = −(1,650 − 1,500) = −150
Net PP&E check: net PP&E goes 1,000 → 1,090, so −[(1,090 − 1,000) + 60] = −150 ✓

Step 5 — CFF

−40 + 100 + 40 − 120 = −20
Notes payable repaid (−40), new long-term debt (+100), stock issued (+40), dividends paid (−120).

Step 6 — tie out

 380 − 150 − 20 = +210 change in cash
=Ending cash = 200 + 210 = 410
How to read it: operations comfortably fund the capital spending, and cash still grows. Dividends are largely covered by CFO rather than by new borrowing. This is the healthy pattern.

08 · What the SCF Can (and Can't) Tell You

Topic 3.8

Two firms can look identical on the surface and be completely different underneath. In the text's comparison, Firm A and Firm B report the same net income ($82M), the same depreciation ($20M), and the same increase in cash (+$15M, from $22M to $37M).

($ millions)Firm AFirm B
CFO+122−22
CFI−41−21
CFF−66+58
Change in cash+15+15
  • Firm A — operations generate cash, and it uses that cash to pay down long-term debt (300 → 234). Negative CFF here is a sign of strength.
  • Firm B — operations consume cash (receivables and inventory ballooned), so it borrowed more (300 → 358) just to stay afloat, and it likely invests less because operations are constraining it.
Why negative CFO is unsustainable. A year or two is not fatal, but over time the funding sources run out: cash balances get used up, assets cannot be sold forever, lenders eventually stop lending, and equity investors lose interest.
The limit of the SCF. One year of data can show that Firm A is sustainable, but it cannot show that Firm A is good. Judging performance requires comparison to competitors and industry benchmarks — which is the job of ratio analysis (Topic 4).

09 · CFO vs. Free Cash Flow

Topic 3.9

Why CFO is not distributable. Equipment and buildings wear out and must be replaced. If an owner took out all of CFO every year, nothing would be left to reinvest and the firm would eventually stop generating CFO at all.

Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash that can be distributed after funding required reinvestment in PP&E and working capital.

MeasureWho it's available toNotes
FCFF
free cash flow to the firm		 All capital providers — debt + equity Most common in analysis; measured before creditors or owners are paid
FCFE
free cash flow to equity		 Equity holders only After debt obligations (interest and principal) and required reinvestment
Dividends Stockholders The cash actually paid out
FCFF — starts from EBIT
FCFF = EBIT × (1 − Tax Rate) + Depreciation − CAPEX − Δ NWC
FCFF starts from EBIT rather than net income because it is measured before creditors and owners are compensated — interest has not been subtracted yet.
FCFE — starts from net income
FCFE = Net Income + Depreciation − CAPEX − Δ NWC + Δ Debt
Net income is already after interest, and new borrowing is cash available to equity holders — so Δ debt is added.
  • NOPAT — net operating profit after taxes = EBIT × (1 − tax rate). That is the first term of FCFF.
  • CAPEX — capital expenditures: spending on fixed assets, often measured as CFI.
  • NWC — net working capital = current assets − current liabilities.
  • Δ Debt — new borrowings − repayments of old debt.

Mini-example (FCFF)

EBIT 500, tax rate 30%, depreciation 80, CAPEX 150, increase in NWC 40:

500(0.70) + 80 − 150 − 40 = 350 + 80 − 150 − 40 = 240
Where this goes next: free cash flow returns later in the course for valuation — it is the cash flow you discount in a discounted cash flow (DCF) model.

10 · The Logan Enterprises Case

Topic 3.1 — the opening case, worked

Given (20X1 → 20X2): net income = 150; no assets sold.

Q1 · How does CFO differ from net income?

Net income is an accrual measure. CFO removes non-cash expenses (depreciation) and reflects the timing differences buried in the operating accounts — receivables, inventory, payables, and accruals.

Q2 · Logan's CFO

Depreciation = 2,500 − 2,400 = 100
CFO = 150 + 100 − 200 (AR) − 300 (inventory) + 200 (AP) + 0 (accruals) = −50

Q3 · Logan's CFI

CFI = −(3,500 − 3,300) = −200

Q4 · Logan's CFF

Dividends = (400 + 150) − 500 = 50
CFF = −100 (notes payable) + 600 (LT debt) + 0 (stock) − 50 (dividends) = +450
Tie-out
−50 − 200 + 450 = +200 = 700 − 500 ✓

Q5 · What does the SCF tell us?

Logan is profitable on paper (net income of 150) but its operations consumed 50 in cash, because receivables and inventory grew faster than payables. Capital spending (200) plus that operating shortfall were funded by 600 in new long-term debt. This is the same warning pattern as Firm B: growth financed by borrowing rather than by operations, which is not sustainable long-term.

12 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The distinctions Topic 3 questions are built on

  • Notes payable belongs in CFF, not CFO — even though it is listed under current liabilities. If it carries interest, it is financing.
  • Never adjust for the change in cash. It is the answer, not an input.
  • Don't forget the depreciation add-back — in CFO, and again when computing CFI from net PP&E.
  • Sign errors. Assets up = cash down; liabilities up = cash up. CFI is normally negative for a growing firm.
  • Dividends aren't listed anywhere. Derive them: Dividends = (Old RE + Net income) − New RE.
  • Gain/loss on a PP&E sale sits in net income but belongs to CFI, not CFO.
  • Positive net income does not guarantee positive CFO. Growth in receivables and inventory can drain cash — that is the Logan / Firm B pattern.
  • Total cash flow is hard to misstate; the split between the three buckets is not. Check the categories, not just the total.
  • Always tie out. If CFO + CFI + CFF ≠ the change in balance-sheet cash, something is misclassified or mis-signed.
  • FCFF starts from EBIT, FCFE from net income. FCFF is measured before creditors and owners are paid; FCFE adds back Δ debt.
  • CFO is not distributable. Assets must be replaced — free cash flow is what's left after required reinvestment.
  • One year of SCF shows sustainability, not quality. Judging whether a firm is good takes ratio analysis (Topic 4).
