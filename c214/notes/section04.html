01 · Why Ratios?

Topic 4.1

A head-to-head comparison of raw financial numbers (revenue, profit, debt) is often meaningless because firms differ in size. Ratio analysis scales one number against an economically relevant variable — for example revenue ÷ assets = asset turnover — so that firms of different sizes can be compared.

1

Standardization

"$1M in debt" means little on its own. Scaled by assets, the debt ratio reveals the relative load — a firm 90% debt-financed looks very different from one that is 20% debt-financed.

2

Flexibility

Unlike GAAP, ratio analysis has no fixed rulebook. Which ratios to use, how many years of data, and how to handle accounting differences are all analyst judgment calls.

3

Focus

Ratios don't tell you what is happening — they tell you what questions to ask. A ratio that shifts over time or departs from industry norms says "investigate here."

Ratios are grouped into four categories:

Liquidity
can I pay?
·
Efficiency
do I use assets well?
·
Financing
who funds me?
·
Profitability
do I earn?
Where set rules apply vs. judgment: the entries on the financial statements themselves follow GAAP guidelines. What the analyst does with them — which ratios, which years, which adjustments — is judgment.

02 · Liquidity Ratios

Topic 4.2 — a firm's ability to meet short-term obligations

RatioFormula
Current RatioCurrent Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Quick Ratio(Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities
  • Current liabilities — obligations requiring cash within one year. Current assets — items generating cash within one year.
  • A higher current ratio means a better likelihood of meeting short-term obligations.
  • The quick ratio strips out inventory — the least liquid current asset — for a stricter test.
  • A liquid asset can be converted to cash quickly without loss of significant value.
Key principle: you cannot judge liquidity from any single ratio. Take an integrated view of several.
Quick-ratio formula note: the official Formula References sheet (Pre-Assessment and OA) uses (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities. Some study materials show (Cash + AR) ÷ Current Liabilities instead — they usually give the same result, but use the Current Assets − Inventory version on the assessment.

Mini-example

Current assets 900, inventory 360, current liabilities 600:

Current = 900 ÷ 600 = 1.50  |  Quick = (900 − 360) ÷ 600 = 0.90

03 · Efficiency Ratios

Topic 4.3 — how effectively management uses assets to generate sales and profits

RatioFormula
Total Asset Turnover (TAT)Sales ÷ Total Assets
Fixed Asset Turnover (FAT)Sales ÷ Fixed Assets
Accounts Receivable (AR) TurnoverCredit Sales ÷ AR
Average Collection Period (ACP)365 ÷ AR Turnover
Inventory TurnoverCOGS ÷ Inventory
Days Inventory on Hand (DOH)365 ÷ Inventory Turnover
  • Reading TAT: a TAT of 3 means $3 of sales per $1 of assets.
  • Why FAT? It removes current assets from the denominator, because current assets reflect management's risk tolerance. That isolates the long-term (fixed) assets the industry requires.
  • Turnover vs. days: turnover is times per year; ACP and DOH convert that to days per turn by dividing 365 by the turnover.
  • Match numerator to denominator: AR is tied to credit sales; inventory is carried at cost, so it is tied to COGS, not sales.
"When is good really bad?" A high TAT is not automatically good. It might mean efficient asset use — or the firm has failed to reinvest in modern equipment and is squeezing sales out of outdated assets. Don't assume higher = better without context.
Accounting distorts efficiency ratios. A firm that depreciates assets more slowly reports higher asset values, so it appears to have lower turnover — even with operations identical to a peer. That is an accounting artifact, not an economic difference.

Mini-example

Credit sales 7,300 with AR 1,000; COGS 8,400 with inventory 1,400:

 AR turnover = 7,300 ÷ 1,000 = 7.3 → ACP = 365 ÷ 7.3 = 50 days
 Inventory turnover = 8,400 ÷ 1,400 = 6.0 → DOH = 365 ÷ 6 ≈ 61 days

04 · Financing (Solvency) Ratios

Topic 4.4 — the proportions of debt vs. equity used to finance assets

Recall the balance sheet identity
Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity
There are only two financing sources, so if you know the debt share, the equity share is whatever is left.
RatioFormulaHow to read it
Debt RatioTotal Liabilities ÷ Total AssetsCents of every asset-dollar that are debt-financed
Debt-to-Equity (D/E)Total Liabilities ÷ Total EquityDollars of debt for every $1 of equity
Times Interest Earned (TIE)EBIT ÷ Interest ExpenseHow many times operating earnings cover the interest bill
Financial Leverage RatioTotal Assets ÷ EquityEquals 1 + D/E
Reading the numbers: a debt ratio of 0.75 means 75¢ of each asset-dollar is debt-financed and the other 25¢ is equity. A D/E of 2 means the firm carries $2 of debt for every $1 of equity.
Why 1 + D/E? Since Assets = Liabilities + Equity, Assets ÷ Equity = (Liabilities + Equity) ÷ Equity = 1 + Liabilities ÷ Equity. This is the leverage term in the DuPont formula.

Mini-example

Total liabilities 1,800; total equity 1,200 (so total assets 3,000); EBIT 840; interest 120:

Debt ratio = 0.60  |  D/E = 1,800 ÷ 1,200 = 1.50  |  Assets ÷ Equity = 2.50 = 1 + 1.50  |  TIE = 840 ÷ 120 = 7.0
Financial risk is the increased volatility in earnings that comes from using debt. Leverage — using cost structure or debt to magnify returns — magnifies risk along with them.

05 · Profitability Ratios

Topic 4.5 — sales-based margins and investment-based returns

Sales-based margins

The numerators trace the flow of the income statement — gross profit → EBIT → net income — so the three margins are usually analyzed together as a set.

RatioFormula
Gross MarginGross Profit ÷ Sales
Operating MarginEBIT ÷ Sales
Net MarginNet Income ÷ Sales
Interpreting margin gaps: if a firm's operating margin trails the industry by the same amount as its gross margin gap, the shortfall traces back to COGS (the top of the income statement) — not to poor operating expense control. Read margins together, not in isolation.

Investment-based returns

RatioFormula
Return on Assets (ROA)Net Income ÷ Total Assets
Return on Equity (ROE)Net Income ÷ Owner's Equity
  • ROA = earnings as a percentage of all the capital entrusted to management; comparable across industries.
  • ROE answers "As an owner, how much did I earn per dollar invested?" It is the ratio managers are most often judged by.
  • Same numerator (net income); the denominators differ by the balance sheet equation. A firm using debt effectively will show ROE > ROA.
  • Returns are the bottom-line measures used to assess and compare company performance.

Mini-example

Sales 8,000; COGS 5,200; operating expenses 1,800; interest 200; tax rate 40%:

 Gross profit = 8,000 − 5,200 = 2,800 → gross margin 35.0%
 EBIT = 2,800 − 1,800 = 1,000 → operating margin 12.5%
 Pre-tax = 1,000 − 200 = 800; tax 320; net income = 480 → net margin 6.0%

06 · The DuPont Decomposition

Topic 4.6 — the "grand-daddy of ratios"

The DuPont formula breaks ROE into profitability, efficiency, and leverage components:

DuPont — three multiplicative drivers
ROE = Net Margin × TAT × (1 + D/E)
= (Net Income ÷ Sales) × (Sales ÷ Total Assets) × (Assets ÷ Equity). The middle terms cancel to leave Net Income ÷ Equity. Assets ÷ Equity = 1 + D/E because Assets = Liabilities + Equity.
Alternate form
ROE = ROA × (1 + D/E)
Because Net Margin × TAT = ROA.

The three levers to raise ROE

1

Higher margins

Cut costs relative to sales.

2

Greater efficiency

Generate more sales per dollar of assets.

3

More leverage

Use more debt relative to equity.

Why it matters — same ROE, different quality

Firm PFirm Q
Net margin5.0%2.5%
TAT2.0x1.0x
(1 + D/E)2.08.0
ROE20%20%
  • Firm P — strong margin and efficiency with modest leverage: a high-quality ROE.
  • Firm Q — weak on both fundamentals; it only matches P by taking on heavy leverage, which makes it much riskier.
Leverage cuts both ways. If the earnings on debt-financed assets fall below the cost of debt, ROE swings hard in the other direction.

Working backwards

ROA 8%, ROE 20% → 1 + D/E = 20 ÷ 8 = 2.5 → D/E = 1.5
Don't forget to subtract the 1 — 2.5 is Assets ÷ Equity, not D/E.

07 · How to Use Ratios

Topic 4.7 — from numbers to information

A ratio is meaningless without a comparison standard. There are three:

StandardCompare the firm to…
Trend analysisits own history — often five years back
Cross-sectional analysispeers, competitors, or industry averages
Internal goal monitoringmanagement's own targets (e.g., an internally set ROE goal)

Two common pitfalls

1

Timing Issues

Mixing income statement and balance sheet data from firms with different fiscal year-ends distorts comparisons — especially for seasonal firms. Two identical swimsuit makers with Dec. 31 vs. June 30 balance sheet dates will report different asset levels and different ROA despite identical performance.

2

Accounting Issues

Accrual accounting allows real variation in reported results (depreciation method, inventory valuation). Understand each firm's accounting choices before assuming ratios are truly comparable.

08 · The Art and Science of Ratios

Topic 4.8 — refinement and adjustment

Before comparing firms, analysts "scrub" (recast) the data. That is the science:

  • Timing differences — align fiscal year-ends across the comparison set.
  • Accounting differences — reconcile things like differing depreciation useful-life assumptions.

The harder, more subjective art is judgment:

  • Which firms belong in the comparison set? The whole industry, direct competitors, or only successful competitors?
  • What time period is relevant? Structural or technological change can make older data useless.
  • Which ratios matter for this industry? The quick ratio is critical for car dealers (big, slow-moving inventory) but less relevant for a high-volume, low-price retailer with liquid, fast-turning inventory.

Worked example — shrinking industry

Firm A's operating margin and TAT fell much more sharply than Firm B's, even though both saw similar gross margin declines from price competition.

  • Cause: Firm A failed to cut operating expenses or reduce/redeploy assets as sales fell.
  • Cure: reduce non-production expenses (labor, supplies) and evaluate underused assets for disposal or redeployment.

09 · Risk, Legality, and Social Responsibility

Topic 4.9 — from insight to wisdom

Ratio analysis is only one piece of a firm's true economic character. Layer it with three macro considerations:

ConsiderationWhat to look at
Risk — external Macroeconomic cycle, competitive forces, technology shifts, demographic and societal change
Risk — internal Financial flexibility, willingness to innovate, compliance culture, company culture. Enron is the classic firm destroyed by internal forces.
Legality Ratios can hint at compliance culture. A management team willing to "stretch" accounting rules to smooth volatility signals a weak compliance culture.
Social responsibility Profitability earned at the expense of employees, community, or environment tends to erode long-term performance. Human capital and community health feed back into productivity and customer relationships.

11 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The distinctions Topic 4 questions are built on

  • Quick ratio: subtract inventory from current assets — don't add it, and don't divide inventory by current liabilities.
  • Turnover → days needs 365 ÷ turnover. Stopping at the turnover, or multiplying by 365, is the classic slip.
  • Inventory pairs with COGS; receivables pair with credit sales. Don't use total sales for either.
  • D/E ≠ debt ratio. D/E divides by equity; the debt ratio divides by total assets.
  • TIE starts at EBIT, not net income — net income is already after interest.
  • DuPont uses (1 + D/E), not D/E. Assets ÷ Equity = 1 + D/E.
  • Working backwards: ROE ÷ ROA = 1 + D/E, then subtract 1 for D/E.
  • Identical ROEs can hide different firms. Break ROE into margin, turnover, and leverage before judging quality.
  • Leverage cuts both ways — it magnifies losses as well as gains.
  • A high TAT is not automatically good; it can mean outdated, under-reinvested assets.
  • Slower depreciation → lower turnover — an accounting artifact, not an economic difference.
  • Same-size margin gaps at gross and operating level point to COGS, not operating expenses.
  • No single ratio settles liquidity — take an integrated view.
  • Ratios tell you what questions to ask, not what is happening.
  • Timing vs. accounting pitfalls: different fiscal year-ends = timing; different depreciation or inventory methods = accounting.
  • Ratio analysis is analyst judgment; statement entries follow GAAP.
