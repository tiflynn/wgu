01 · What Is Finance?

Topic 1.1

Finance, in one line
Finance = the study of the management (allocation) of capital
Capital is a financial asset a firm can use — cash, machinery, and the like. Finance is the discipline of deciding where that capital should go.

The three main areas of finance:

1

Corporate Finance

The finance function inside a business. Focuses on financial decision-making by the firm's own management — which projects to fund, how to raise the money.

2

Investments

Understanding financial instruments — stocks and bonds — and how to value them. Which assets to invest in, and what they are actually worth.

3

Financial Institutions

Intermediaries such as banks that link savers to borrowers. A bank profits by paying depositors a lower rate than it charges borrowers.

Memory tip: C-I-B — Corporate finance (inside the firm), Investments (valuing the instruments), Banking (connecting savers and borrowers).
How a bank actually makes money: it is a middleman on interest. Pay the depositor 1%, charge the borrower 6%, keep the spread. That spread is the whole business model — the same idea you will see again with the bid-ask spread in Topic 1.5.

03 · Primary Financial Markets

Topic 1.3

Primary market
Where securities are first sold
Issuers (firms) and buyers (investors) transact directly. This is the only point at which the company itself receives money from the security.

The syndicate

A syndicate is a group of intermediaries — typically large investment banks or institutional investors — temporarily formed to oversee the issuance of a stock or bond.

  • Syndicate members are often also the underwriters of the issue.
  • An underwriter determines the value of the security, then — in most cases — purchases the entire issue from the issuer and resells it to investors.
Why "temporarily formed" matters: a syndicate is assembled for one issuance and dissolves afterward. It is not a standing institution, which is exactly what makes it a syndicate rather than a firm.

How bonds are placed with a syndicate

MethodHow it works
Competitive sale Underwriters submit bids on price and interest rate. The firm selects the underwriter offering the highest price and lowest interest rate.
Negotiated sale Underwriters submit proposals/bids, but the process involves a more thorough interview. The firm carefully selects its underwriting management team rather than just taking the best bid.
Memory tip: Competitive = the numbers decide. Negotiated = the conversation decides.

Stock issuance (IPO)

  • A firm selling shares of ownership for the first time performs an Initial Public Offering (IPO).
  • Stock issuance follows underwriting procedures similar to bonds.
Underwriting fees — know these two numbers
Bonds ≈ 2%  |  Stock ≈ 7%
Stock costs more to underwrite because it is riskier and harder to price, and far more labor-intensive to place.
Don't flip these. The riskier, harder-to-value instrument (stock) carries the higher fee. A bond has a known coupon and maturity; a stock's value is a judgment call.

04 · Secondary Financial Markets

Topic 1.4

Secondary market
Where securities trade after the initial issuance
The company is not involved and receives no proceeds from these trades. This is what people commonly call "the stock market."
The single most common mix-up in Topic 1. When you buy Apple stock from another investor, Apple gets nothing. The firm only raised money in the primary market. Secondary trading moves ownership between investors.

Why NYSE is still an "auction market" even though it's electronic: "auction" describes how prices are determined — competing bids and asks matching — not whether trading happens on a physical floor. NASDAQ, by contrast, relies on dealers actively quoting prices.
Memory tip: Auction = bidders compete. Dealer = dealers compete. On the NYSE the investors set the price; on NASDAQ the dealers post it.

05 · The Role of Financial Markets

Topic 1.5

What financial markets do
Reduce the cost of financing for firms  +  provide liquidity to traders
Liquidity = the ability to convert an asset to cash quickly without losing significant value.

The specialist (NYSE)

  • A market maker on the NYSE who holds an inventory of a particular stock and acts as the liquidity provider.
  • Hired by the NYSE to maintain a "fair and orderly" market — must be willing to buy from sellers and sell to buyers.
  • Because the specialist bears risk (holding inventory in a fluctuating stock), they charge a slightly higher ask and pay a slightly lower bid.
Bid-ask spread — worked example
Ask $100.03 − Bid $99.97 = $0.06 spread
On a stock trading around $100, the specialist sells to you at $100.03 and buys from you at $99.97. That six-cent spread is their compensation for bearing inventory risk.

Direction trap: the ask is always the higher number and the bid the lower one. You buy at the ask and sell at the bid — the spread works against you in both directions, which is exactly why it is the market maker's pay.

06 · Trading in Financial Markets

Topic 1.6

Trading volume has grown enormously over the decades, and electronic trading has steadily reduced the specialist's role along with it.

Order types

Order typeSensitivityDescription
Market order Time-sensitive Executes immediately ("first come, first served") at the current ask/bid. Price is not guaranteed.
Limit order Price-sensitive Executes only when the market reaches the specified "limit" price. Execution is not guaranteed — it may be delayed, or never happen.
Memory tip: Market order buys time; limit order buys price. You always give one up to get the other — nobody gets both guaranteed.
Worked example. Stock trading at $100 (ask $100.03 / bid $99.97).
  • A market order to buy executes immediately at $100.03.
  • A limit order to buy at $100.00 won't execute until the ask drops to $100.00 — or until another investor places a matching limit sell order at $100.00, at which point the two orders "cross" and execute without the specialist quoting a different price.

Why liquidity matters

  • More liquid markets produce more reliable stock prices as signals of value.
  • A stock that trades rarely can carry a "stale" price that doesn't reflect current information.
  • Liquidity therefore benefits both (1) the cost of financing for firms and (2) the reliability of price signals for everyone.

07 · The Goal of the Firm

Topic 1.7

Traditional goal of the firm
Maximize shareholder value — not simply maximize net income
Achieved through profitable decision-making: investing capital in projects that increase the firm's stock price, and avoiding investments that cost more than they return.
Classic distractor: "maximize net income" or "maximize profit" will show up as a wrong answer. Net income is an accounting figure for one period; shareholder value reflects the whole future of the firm. A decision can raise this year's income and still destroy value.
  • Management decisions — new products, acquisitions, and the like — affect the stock price by creating new information for the market.
  • A shareholder is an individual or entity that holds a share (a fraction of ownership) in a company.

09 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The distinctions Topic 1 questions are built on

  • Primary vs. secondary market — the firm receives proceeds only in the primary market. Secondary trades move ownership between investors; the company gets nothing.
  • Bondholders outrank shareholders in liquidation — and shareholders cannot force a bankruptcy. Lenders before owners.
  • Underwriting fees: ~2% bonds, ~7% stock — the harder-to-value, riskier instrument costs more to place. Don't reverse them.
  • Competitive sale = best bid wins; negotiated sale = interview process — one is decided on numbers, the other on the relationship.
  • "Auction market" is about price discovery, not a physical floor — NYSE is electronic now and is still an auction market, because orders compete to set the price.
  • NYSE = one specialist; NASDAQ = multiple competing dealers — and competition is what narrows the NASDAQ spread.
  • Ask is high, bid is low — you buy at the ask, sell at the bid, and the difference pays the market maker for bearing inventory risk.
  • Market order = guaranteed execution, unguaranteed price. Limit order = guaranteed price, unguaranteed execution. Never both.
  • The goal is maximizing shareholder value, not net income — an accounting profit for one period is not the same as value created for owners.
  • A syndicate is temporary — formed for a single issuance, then dissolved.
