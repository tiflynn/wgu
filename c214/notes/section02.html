01 · The Three Financial Statements

Topic 2.1

Each statement describes a different aspect of a firm's economic character. The first thing to get straight is what span of time each one covers — that single distinction drives most of the confusion in this topic.

StatementWhat it showsTime span
Income Statement
a.k.a. P&L		 Results of operations — starts with sales, subtracts expenses, ends with net income Over a period — a video camera recording performance
Balance Sheet Assets, liabilities, and equity — what the firm owns and how it financed those things A single point in time — a still photo of what the firm has right now
Statement of Cash Flows
SCF — covered in Topic 3		 All cash moving into and out of the firm Over a period
Memory tip: Video vs. photo. The income statement rolls film across a quarter or a year; the balance sheet is a shutter click on one date. Ask "as of when?" and you will always know which statement you are looking at.
Why does a finance course start with accounting? Because accountants control the data. To analyze a company you first have to understand its financial position and its operations — and that means reading its financial statements.

02 · Finance vs. Accounting

Topic 2.1

The one distinction that matters
Accounting looks backward  |  Finance looks forward
Accounting reports what already happened. Finance is concerned with liquidity and with providing capital for what comes next.

Financial statements are a starting line, not a finish line. They are an important data source for financial analysis — but analyzing the past is only a springboard to managing the future. Treating the statements as the endpoint is the error.

03 · Accrual Accounting & the Matching Principle

Topic 2.3

Two definitions to lock down
Accrual accounting = historical prices + the matching principle
Accrual accounting records accounts at historical prices and applies the matching principle. In this course it is used interchangeably with GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles). The matching principle means revenues and expenses are recognized in the same period.
Cash accountingAccrual accounting
Revenue recorded When cash comes in When the earnings process is complete and receipt of payment is likely
Expense recorded When cash goes out Matched to the revenue it helped generate

Worked example — the chair company

A firm spends $75,000 in 20X1 to produce 1,000 chairs but sells none. In 20X2 it produces nothing and sells all 1,000 chairs for $100 each — $100,000.

Cash accounting20X120X2
Revenue$0$100,000
− Cost75,0000
Net Income−$75,000$100,000
Accrual accounting20X120X2
Revenue$0$100,000
− Cost075,000
Net Income$0$25,000
What the example proves: cash accounting throws off wild swings — a $75,000 loss followed by a $100,000 gain — that spread the real $25,000 profit arbitrarily across two periods. Accrual accounting matches the $75,000 cost to the $100,000 revenue in the period the sale actually happened, giving a far more accurate picture of operations.
The judgment call hiding inside "earned." Management has considerable discretion over when revenue is earned. Is it when the order is taken? When the product ships? When payment arrives? When the customer is satisfied? Each choice moves revenue between periods.

04 · The Income Statement

Topics 2.4 & 2.5

Basic equation
Revenue − Expenses = Net Income

The standard layout, top to bottom:

 Revenues
−Cost of Goods Sold
=Gross Profit
−Operating Expenses (wages, rent, non-direct labor, office & clerical, depreciation)
=EBIT (Earnings Before Interest and Taxes)
−Interest Expense
=EBT (Earnings Before Taxes)
−Tax Expense
=Net Income

Three ways to think about "income"

TypeWhat it isManagerial discretion
Cash-based income Informal: cash in minus cash out. Close cousin of CFO (cash flow from operations), covered in Topic 3. Least
Income for tax purposes The government's own definition of income, used to compute tax owed. Fewer managerial choices than accounting income
Accounting income Net income as reported on the income statement, calculated under accrual accounting (GAAP). Best metric for understanding operations. Most — and the most complicated

Line-by-line hazards

  • Revenue — recognition timing is a judgment call. Contract signed, product shipped, payment received: which month counts? Management's discretion here is significant.
  • EBIT ("ee-bit," a.k.a. operating profit) — Gross Profit minus Operating Expenses. Frequently used as a proxy for operating income.
  • Interest Expense — sits between EBIT and EBT. Sometimes interest actually paid isn't reported here, and sometimes interest that was never paid still is.
  • Tax Expense — second-to-last line before net income. Firms keep one set of books for the IRS and another for everyone else.
  • Net Income — EBIT minus interest minus taxes. Every line feeding it is subject to accounting assumptions, timing differences, subjectivity, and occasionally outright deceit.
The four "≠ cash" traps. None of these reported figures equals the cash that actually moved:
  • Revenue ≠ cash collected
  • COGS & operating expenses ≠ cash paid
  • Interest expense ≠ interest paid
  • Tax expense ≠ tax paid
The line to remember: "Cash flow is a fact, while net income is only an opinion." Cash either moved or it didn't. Net income is the product of a long chain of choices about timing and estimation.

05 · The Balance Sheet — Structure

Topic 2.6

The fundamental equation
Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity
Commonly rearranged as Equity = Assets − Liabilities. The left side is what the firm owns; the right side is how it financed those things — every asset is paid for either by borrowing (liabilities) or by investing (equity).

Historical cost vs. fair value

The historical cost principle: assets and liabilities are recorded at their cost when acquired, and are not updated for changes in market value. Land bought in 1972 for $1 million still appears at $1 million today, whatever it would actually fetch.
Why one misstatement is never alone. Because the balance sheet must always balance, a misstatement of value on the asset side implies an offsetting misstatement somewhere on the financing side. This is why analysts sometimes "recast" financial statements to better reflect economic reality.

06 · The Asset Side

Topic 2.7

Current assets — listed in order of liquidity

Cash, or assets convertible to cash within a year. Liquidity is how easily an asset converts to cash without losing value — and the statement lists them most liquid first:

Cash
→
Marketable
Securities
→
Accounts
Receivable
→
Inventory
  • Cash — currency, bills, coins, checking deposits. The most liquid current asset.
  • Marketable Securities — short-term, high-quality securities such as T-bills and CDs.
  • Accounts Receivable (AR) — cash owed to the firm by customers for goods or services already delivered but not yet paid for. Management discretion in recognizing bad debt affects how "real" the AR balance actually is.
  • Inventory — raw materials, work-in-process, and finished goods. The least liquid current asset, exposed to salability, spoilage, and shrinkage risk, plus management discretion in valuation.

Fixed assets (PP&E)

Property, plant & equipment — assets with a useful life of more than one year, normally recorded at historical cost.

Net PP&E (book value)
Gross PP&E − Accumulated Depreciation = Net PP&E
Gross PP&E is the original cost of all non-current assets held for use. Accumulated depreciation is the cumulative total of all depreciation claimed against it.
Depreciation expense vs. accumulated depreciation. A $100 asset with a 5-year life might carry $20/year of depreciation expense — that lands on the income statement, one period at a time. The running total of every dollar of depreciation ever claimed against that asset is accumulated depreciation, and that lives on the balance sheet.
Book value is not market value. Net PP&E can diverge significantly from an asset's actual fair value, because depreciation schedules rarely mirror true economic depreciation.

07 · The Financing Side

Topic 2.8

Current liabilities — listed in order of maturity

Obligations due within a year, shortest maturity first.

ItemWhat it isCarries interest?
Accounts Payable (AP) Money owed to suppliers for purchases made on credit — the mirror image of AR No — typically interest-free
Notes Payable (NP) Short-term borrowed money, typically under 5 years Yes — NP does carry interest expense
Accruals Expenses incurred but not yet paid — wages earned in March but paid April 10 show as "accrued wages" on the March 31 balance sheet No
AP vs. NP is the trap here. Both are short-term obligations sitting next to each other, but only notes payable costs you interest. Owing your supplier is free; owing the bank is not.

Long-term liabilities are debt obligations maturing in more than a year. Their stated balance sheet value can differ substantially from market value.

Equity — the three components

1

Common Stock (CS)

Equity sold to common shareholders at par value — often just $1/share. Common shareholders are last in line in bankruptcy.

2

Additional Paid-In Capital

APIC — the proceeds from issuing stock minus the par value of that stock. The premium investors paid above par.

3

Retained Earnings (RE)

Cumulative net income not paid out as dividends over the entire life of the firm.

Retained earnings is not cash. RE has already been reinvested into the firm's asset base — it cannot be used to pay bills. A company with $50 million in retained earnings may have almost no cash. This is one of the two biggest conceptual traps in Topic 2.
Equity absorbs the errors. Since A = L + OE, any misstatement on the asset or liability side gets absorbed here — which is why the book value of equity often bears little resemblance to the market value of the company's equity.

08 · Linking the Statements

Topic 2.9

Net income can only go two places: out the door as dividends, or retained inside the firm.

Step 1 — where net income goes
Net Income = Dividends + ΔRE
Step 2 — this year's balance sheet from last year's
New RE = Old RE + ΔRE
Step 3 — substitute, and you get the form you will actually use
New RE = Old RE + Net Income − Dividends
This is the bridge between the income statement and the balance sheet, and you will see it repeatedly for the rest of the course — including in forecasting. Rearrange it to solve for whichever piece the question withholds.
Solving for dividends: Dividends = Old RE + Net Income − New RE. If you have two years of retained earnings and one year of net income, the dividend figure falls right out.

09 · The ElectroCar Case

Topic 2.2 — the running example

Jimmy Aweshock, a new equity analyst at High Return, LLC (a private equity growth fund), is handed two years of ElectroCar's financial statements and asked to evaluate the company. ElectroCar is a growth-stage firm: revenue roughly doubled year over year, but so did its losses.

What the numbers reveal — each one is an application of a concept above (figures in $1,000s):

  • Revenue doubled, gross profit halved. Revenue rose from $204,242 to $413,256, but cost of goods sold rose faster — so gross profit fell from $61,595 to $30,067. Growing sales does not mean growing profit.
  • Dividends were essentially zero. Retained earnings fell from $(669,392) to $(1,065,606) — a drop of $396,214 — against a net loss of $396,213. Run it through New RE = Old RE + NI − Dividends and the entire change is explained by the loss alone. A money-losing growth company pays no dividends.
  • Accounts payable exploded. AP rose from $88,250 to $343,180 — an increase of $254,930. A jump that size means the firm is stretching its suppliers to conserve cash, which is a warning sign about its cash position.
  • Long-term debt rose $140,295. From $271,165 to $411,460 — the company is funding its losses by borrowing.
  • Cash fell while inventory ballooned. Cash dropped $57,758 while inventory rose $218,422. Cash is being converted into unsold product.
  • Accruals are everywhere. Receivables ($26,842) and payables ($343,180) are both accruals — revenue recorded before cash arrives, and expenses recorded before cash leaves. That $413,256 of revenue is emphatically not $413,256 of collected cash.
The takeaway: ElectroCar is the whole topic in one company. Accruals separate reported figures from cash, the balance sheet shows how the losses were financed, and the RE linkage ties the two statements together.

11 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The distinctions Topic 2 questions are built on

  • Tax expense ≠ taxes actually paid. Firms keep one set of books for the IRS and another for everyone else. The same goes for revenue vs. cash collected, expenses vs. cash paid, and interest expense vs. interest paid.
  • Retained earnings is not cash. It is already reinvested in the asset base and cannot pay a bill. Never treat RE as a pile of money.
  • Income statement = a period. Balance sheet = a point in time. Video camera vs. still photo.
  • Accounts payable is free; notes payable is not. Only NP carries interest expense.
  • Inventory is the least liquid current asset, cash the most. The order is Cash → Marketable Securities → AR → Inventory.
  • Depreciation expense is one period on the income statement; accumulated depreciation is the lifetime total on the balance sheet.
  • Book value ≠ market value — for fixed assets (depreciation schedules aren't economic reality) and for equity (it absorbs every misstatement elsewhere).
  • New RE = Old RE + Net Income − Dividends. Rearrange for whichever term the question leaves out — usually dividends.
  • Accounting looks backward; finance looks forward. Statements are a springboard for managing the future, not the endpoint of analysis.
  • Accrual accounting = historical prices + matching, and in this course it is interchangeable with GAAP.
  • Income for tax purposes involves fewer managerial choices than accounting income — accounting income is the most discretionary of the three.
