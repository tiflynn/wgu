01 · Management vs. Financial Accounting

Topics 9.1 & 9.2

The one-line distinction
Management Accounting = a COMPETITIVE TOOL  |  Financial Accounting = an UNBIASED SCOREBOARD
Management accounting is detailed data used inside the business for daily decisions. Financial accounting is summary data used by outsiders (banks, investors) making occasional decisions — should we lend to them? invest in them? Good management accounting should never be about deceiving anyone; good financial accounting should never be used to try to gain a competitive edge.
Management AccountingFinancial Accounting
SourceEvolves from the best practices of companies working to be competitive — nimble, no permission neededLegislated and governed by regulatory agencies and professional institutions — a legal/rules-based process
PurposeServes the competitive needs of organizations working to uniquely serve specific customers in specific marketsServes the need for organizations to periodically report results to outside investors and lenders, consistently and comparably
Type of dataBoth financial and nonfinancial (e.g., employee turnover %, customer satisfaction %)Restricted to financial (dollar-denominated) data only
AvailabilityProprietary — kept secret from competitors and the publicOften made public — reported consistently to investors & creditors
Used forInternal planning, control, and evaluationDeciding whether to provide capital (loans, investment) to the company
Restaurant example: two soup-and-salad restaurants. Restaurant #1 just gets the bills and payroll paid on time — routine bookkeeping. Restaurant #2 tracks item-by-item cost and price, customer buying patterns, and frequent combos, then uses all of it to run targeted promotions and beat the competition. Same industry, same product — the difference is entirely in how each one uses its management accounting data.

02 · Standard-Setters & Professional Bodies

Topic 9.2

Financial accounting needs a common reporting platform so outsiders can compare companies on equal footing — that's the whole reason GAAP exists.

OrganizationRole
SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission)U.S. government regulator with the actual legal authority over accounting rules — delegates day-to-day rule-writing to the FASB
FASB (Financial Accounting Standards Board)Writes U.S. GAAP, under SEC oversight
IASB (International Accounting Standards Board)Sets accounting rules internationally, outside the U.S.
IMA (Institute of Management Accountants)Sets professional standards for accountants working inside companies; issues the CMA credential
CPA vs. CMA: a CPA is generally thought of as an external-facing credential (though an internal accountant can hold one too). The CMA, via the IMA, is built specifically around the competencies needed to operate as a management accountant inside a company.

03 · The Management Process: Plan → Control → Evaluate

Topic 9.2 — Figure 9.1

Planning
→
Controlling
→

→
(back to Planning)

Each phase has its own sub-steps. Planning and Controlling are sequential processes; Evaluating is more a set of related activities:

P

Planning

Outlining activities needed to hit objectives. Order: Recognize needs → Identify alternatives → Evaluate choices → Implement decisions.

C

Controlling

Implementing the plan & tracking actual vs. plan. Order: Establish expectations → Create performance measures → Gather results → Compute variances.

E

Evaluating

Analyzing results at period-end: Reward performance, Provide feedback, Analyze results, Identify problems — which then feeds the next planning cycle.

Baseball radar gun analogy (Controlling): you don't just send a pitcher out and hope. You track pitch speed in real time against the expected speed, pitch by pitch. When something drifts off-plan — a variance — that's your signal to pay attention and intervene. Most of controlling is about not watching the things that are already on track, and focusing only where a variance shows up.

04 · Long-Run vs. Short-Run Planning

Topic 9.2

Long-Run Planning (3–5 years)Short-Run Planning (this year or less)
Sub-types Strategic planning — broad, long-range, set by top management: which direction, which markets, which customers.

Capital budgeting — systematic planning for long-term investments in operating assets (equipment, facilities, key hires).		 Production & process prioritizing — continual evaluation of product line/division profitability to spot opportunities.

Operational budgeting (profit planning) — regular, frequent planning for current/near-future operations.
Memorable line: "A company without a budget is like driving with the windshield covered — everything is a surprise." Budgeting lets you foresee problems on paper and solve them before they happen.

05 · CVP Terms & Breakeven Formulas

Topic 9.3

TermDefinitionExample
Variable costIncreases the more you make or sellHamburger meat: 0 burgers = $0, 1,000 burgers = $500
Fixed costStays the same no matter how much you make or sellRestaurant rent: $2,300/month whether you sell 1 burger or 1,000
Careful — total vs. per-unit: total fixed cost never changes with volume, but fixed cost per unit DOES change — it decreases as volume increases, because the same total is spread across more units ($5,000 rent ÷ 2,000 units = $2.50/unit, but ÷ 3,000 units = only $1.67/unit).
Units to Break Even Not on the official formula sheet — memorize directly
Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution Margin per unit
Contribution margin per unit = Selling price − Variable cost per unit. Example (Lily Company): Price $40, VC $24 → CM = $16/unit. Fixed Costs $48,000 ÷ $16 = 3,000 units to break even.
Sales $ to Break Even Also missing from the formula sheet
Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution Margin Ratio
CM ratio = CM per unit ÷ Selling price. Example (same Lily data): CM ratio = $16 ÷ $40 = 40%. $48,000 ÷ 0.40 = $120,000 in sales to break even. Sanity check: 3,000 units × $40 = $120,000 — same answer either way.
Formula sheet shortcut: the official sheet already shows "Contribution Margin per unit" and "Contribution Margin Ratio" at the bottom of the last page — it's just missing the words "Fixed costs /" in front of both lines. Remember to add that, and you've got both breakeven formulas.

06 · Product Costs vs. Period Costs

Topic 9.3

Product Costs (Manufacturing)Period Costs (Non-Manufacturing)
Where incurredInside the production facility (the factory)Outside the production facility
Becomes an expenseWhen the product is sold — first sits on the balance sheet as inventory, then flows to the income statement as Cost of Goods SoldIn the period it's incurred — regardless of whether anything sold
ExamplesWages of production workers, material costs, rent/depreciation/insurance on production assetsCompany HQ costs, selling costs (commissions, advertising), interest

Product costs break into three specific subtypes:

1

Direct Materials

The primary raw material that becomes the product. Example: the potatoes used to make french fries.

2

Direct Labor

Wages of workers directly assembling the product. Example: auto assembly line workers building a car.

3

Manufacturing Overhead

Every other factory cost — not direct materials, not direct labor. Examples: supervisor salaries, factory depreciation, indirect materials like glue or screws.

Exam trap — location, not direct/indirect, drives product vs. period: indirect labor (like a factory supervisor) is easy to mistake for a period cost because it's "indirect." But it's still physically inside the factory, so it's a product cost — specifically, part of manufacturing overhead. Ask "inside the factory or outside?" first — direct/indirect is a separate, second question.

07 · Decision-Making Cost Pairs

Topic 9.3 — three pairs, each tied to a type of business question

Business questionTerm pairWhat it means
Should we stop making this product / close this division? Direct vs. Indirect Direct = specifically traceable to the segment; disappears if the segment is dropped. Indirect = a common/joint cost (e.g., corporate HQ) that benefits several segments and remains even if one segment is dropped.
Should we do Action A (drop a line, fire someone, take a special order)? Differential vs. Sunk Differential = a future cost that changes based on the decision (also called incremental/relevant). Sunk = a past cost that can't be changed by any decision now — "spilled milk." Ignore sunk costs when deciding, even though it's psychologically hard to do.
What is the true full cost of a choice? Out-of-pocket vs. Opportunity Out-of-pocket = an actual outlay of cash or another asset. Opportunity = the benefit forfeited by NOT choosing an alternative — a real economic cost, but easy to overlook since no cash changes hands.
Central Park example (Out-of-pocket vs. Opportunity): NYC's out-of-pocket cost to maintain Central Park is landscaping, mowing, water management — real, but relatively small. The far bigger cost is the opportunity cost: the billions in rent NYC forgoes by not leasing that land for commercial development. It's easy to ignore because no check gets written for it — but rational decision-makers weigh both.

The controller is the chief (head) accountant in most organizations, reporting up through the CFO.

What Sarbanes-Oxley requires (ethics angle)
CEO/CFO personal certification  |  Code of ethics  |  Internal control
The CEO and CFO must personally certify that public financial statements are accurate — creating legal, not just moral, accountability. Public companies must maintain a code of ethics, reviewed by the external auditor, and maintain internal control to ensure ethical, organized operations. (See Section 9 notes for SOX's auditor-specific requirements and the PCAOB.)

The IMA's four ethical responsibilities for management accountants:

1

Competence

Maintain your professional credentials and training — stay current in your field.

2

Confidentiality

Never disclose the company's proprietary internal information to outsiders.

3

Integrity

Act with integrity in all situations — you're in a position of trust and power.

4

Objectivity / Credibility

Report information to decision-makers without bias — don't selectively reveal or conceal to steer a decision.

09 · Handling Ethical Dilemmas

Topic 9.4

If you spot an ethical problem inside your company, the recommended escalation path stays internal as long as possible — you owe the company loyalty, since it pays your salary:

1st
Immediate supervisor
Your default first move
2nd
Next level up
Only if the supervisor IS the problem
3rd
Confidential mentor
An objective advisor inside the company
4th
Resign
Absolute last resort
Personal ethical bedrock: "I will not use accounting to deceive others." Since a management accountant controls the flow of internal information, that access is real power — and the core commitment has to be a flat refusal to use it to mislead anyone.

Examples of internal ethical dilemmas named in the notes:

  • Padding expense accounts (fake receipts)
  • Theft, embezzlement, or shoplifting
  • Inflating profits on financial reports (e.g., to boost a bonus)
  • Violating the firm's purchasing policies (steering business to friends)
  • Using company assets for personal use
  • Kickbacks — a vendor pays you personally (not the company) for steering business their way; that's what separates a kickback from a legitimate rebate
  • Adjusting or manipulating reports, or withholding unfavorable information

10 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The mix-ups that show up most often

  • Financial accounting is NEVER a competitive tool — it's an unbiased scoreboard. Only management accounting is a competitive tool.
  • Fixed cost TOTAL vs. PER UNIT — total fixed cost never changes with volume; fixed cost per unit decreases as volume rises. Don't mix these up.
  • Breakeven formulas aren't on the official formula sheet — Fixed Costs ÷ CM per unit (units) and Fixed Costs ÷ CM Ratio (sales $). Memorize both directly.
  • Product vs. Period is about LOCATION, not direct/indirect — indirect labor (e.g., a factory supervisor) is still a product cost (manufacturing overhead) because it happens inside the factory, even though it's "indirect."
  • Direct/Indirect costs and dropping a segment — direct costs disappear if the segment is dropped; indirect (common/joint) costs remain, since they were never really caused by that one segment.
  • Sunk costs should be IGNORED in decision-making, even though it's tempting to weigh them (this is part of why housing markets adjust slowly — sellers anchor on what they originally paid).
  • Opportunity cost is a real economic cost — even though no cash changes hands, rational decision-makers still have to factor it in (the Central Park example).
  • Kickback vs. rebate — a rebate goes to the company; a kickback goes to the individual employee personally. That's what makes it unethical/fraudulent.
  • SOX ethics requirements are personal, not just corporate — the CEO and CFO personally certify the financial statements; it's not just "the company" that's on the hook.
  • Escalation order for ethical problems — supervisor → next level up (only if supervisor is the problem) → confidential mentor → resign (last resort). Whistleblowing publicly first is NOT the recommended first move.
