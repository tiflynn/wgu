01 · Purpose of the Statement of Cash Flows

Topic 5.1

What it reports
Cash inflow + Cash outflow, with emphasis on CASH GENERATED BY OPERATIONS
Has been required in the United States since 1988. It exists as a companion to the balance sheet and income statement, providing information that isn't readily apparent from either one alone.

Why was it created? Because net income sometimes fails to reflect a company's true performance. The cash flow statement is the reality check.

Operating Activities
The daily grind — why we're in business
+
Investing Activities
Expanding productive capacity
+
Financing Activities
Cash to/from owners & creditors
Everything on one page: the cash flow statement is a one-page summary of the results of a company's operating, investing, and financing activities for the period.

02 · Three Scenarios Where Net Income Fails

Topic 5.1 — the core justification for why this statement exists

1

Big Loss Scenario — too pessimistic

A large net loss from an asset impairment writedown reflects a real decline in value, but since the writedown is a non-cash expense, it doesn't hurt short-term liquidity or operating cash flow. Net income looks terrible while cash flow stays healthy.

2

Rapid Growth Scenario — too optimistic

Accrual-based revenue recognition can make net income look strong even as operating cash flow goes negative. Growth demands upfront cash for inventory & capex, while credit sales delay collection. A company can be profitable and still hit a liquidity crisis.

3

Reality Check Scenario — too "iffy"

Net income relies on estimates and assumptions (like useful life for depreciation) that management has some latitude to adjust — especially under pressure to look good for financing or investors. Operating cash flow reflects actual transactions, so it checks the credibility of earnings.

Key vocabulary from this topic
Asset impairment writedown — reducing the recorded (book) value of a long-term asset because it's no longer worth what's on the books.

Non-cash expense — an expense that reduces net income but involves no actual cash outflow.
Forecasting tool: constructing a pro forma (projected) cash flow statement lets you analyze whether operating, investing, and financing plans are consistent and workable before they happen. More on this in section 09 below.

03 · Cash & Cash Equivalents

Topic 5.2

The two-part test
Readily convertible to a KNOWN amount of cash + Close enough to maturity (≈3 months or less) that value risk is insignificant
Only items passing both parts count as a cash equivalent. Not every current asset qualifies — receivables and inventory are current assets but are NOT cash equivalents.
QualifiesDoes NOT qualify
Treasury bills — short-term, U.S.-government-backed, extremely liquidNotes receivable — a company-specific IOU carrying credit risk and often a longer maturity
Checking accounts — cash on demandLong-term investment securities
Money market funds — highly liquid, stable valueInventory
Exam trap: "notes receivable" is the classic wrong-answer bait in a cash-equivalents question. It fails the test on both liquidity (not readily convertible on demand) and risk (the debtor might not pay).

04 · The Three Categories: Operating / Investing / Financing

Topic 5.2

The core question for every cash transaction: "What relationship does this cash flow represent?"

CategoryDefinitionCash ReceiptsCash Payments
Operating Transactions relating to delivering/producing goods or providing services — activities that enter into the calculation of net income Sale of goods/services · Sale of trading securities · Interest revenue · Dividend revenue Inventory purchases · Wages & salaries · Taxes · Interest expense · Other expenses (utilities, rent) · Purchase of trading securities
Investing (1) Acquiring/selling productive assets (PP&E), (2) acquiring/selling investment securities, (3) lending money and collecting on loans Sale of plant assets · Sale of a business segment · Sale of nontrading securities · Collection of loan principal Purchase of plant assets · Purchase of nontrading securities · Making loans to other entities
Financing Obtaining resources from owners & providing a return; obtaining resources from creditors & repaying borrowings Issuance of stock · Borrowing (bonds, notes, mortgages) Cash dividends · Repayment of loans (principal) · Repurchase of stock (treasury stock)
Trading vs. nontrading securities twist: trading securities are bought/sold as part of routine activity, so they stay in operating. Nontrading (investment) securities belong in investing. Same asset type, different classification, depending on intent.
Net cash provided by operating activities
= the "bottom line" of the cash flow statement
Just as net income is the bottom line of the income statement, operating cash flow is the headline number of the cash flow statement — long-run survival depends on operations generating cash.

05 · The GAAP "Gotcha" Rules

Topic 5.2/5.3 — controversial classifications that don't follow the logic above

Why "gotcha"? Interest and dividends relate to debt/investment relationships (which sound like financing or investing), but GAAP puts most of them in operating instead — because they flow through the income statement in computing net income.
Item (U.S. GAAP)ClassificationWhy
Cash paid for interestOperatingInterest expense enters net income
Cash received for interestOperatingInterest revenue enters net income
Cash received for dividendsOperatingDividend revenue enters net income
Cash paid for income taxesOperatingTax expense enters net income
Cash dividends PAID to stockholdersFinancingThe one exception — a distribution of profits to owners, not an income-statement item
Repayment of loan principalFinancingReturning borrowed capital to a creditor (contrast with the interest on that same loan, which is operating)
Purchase of treasury stock (your own)FinancingA transaction with your own owners
Memory rule: "Income items → operating. Capital moves → financing." Interest (both directions) and dividends received all touch the income statement. Dividends paid, loan principal, and stock buybacks all touch the capital structure instead.

One loan payment can split into two sections:

Loan payment = Principal + Interest
Principal portion → Financing  |  Interest portion → Operating
Even though both pieces leave the bank account together, the statement of cash flows splits them into different sections.
ItemU.S. GAAPInternational (IFRS)
Cash paid for interestOperating onlyOperating or Financing
Cash received for interest/dividendsOperating onlyOperating or Investing
Cash paid for income taxesOperating onlyOperating, Investing, or Financing
Whose stock? Buying another company's stock as an investment → Investing. Buying back your own company's stock (treasury stock) → Financing.

06 · How the Statements Connect & Cash Flow Patterns

Topic 5.2

Operating Cash Flow
+ accrual adjustments →
Net Income

A company generates cash from day-to-day operations. Accountants then apply accrual adjustments — did you actually earn the revenue yet, even if paid in advance? Did employees work but not get paid yet? These adjustments turn raw cash flow into the refined measure of performance: net income. The two are tightly connected, differing only by accrual accounting.

Each section of the cash flow statement matches up with the corresponding part of the balance sheet — operating with current assets/liabilities, investing with long-term assets, financing with debt and equity.

CategoryNormal / healthy signWhat it signals
OperatingPositiveA healthy business generates cash from its core activity
InvestingNegativeMost companies are spending cash to expand or enhance long-term assets
FinancingDepends on growth speedPositive = young, rapidly expanding company needing external financing. Negative = mature company returning cash (dividends, debt repayment, buybacks) to its capital providers
Non-cash investing & financing activities: some investing/financing activities affect a company's financial position but involve no cash during the period — e.g., purchasing a long-term asset by issuing debt or stock directly to the seller. Since no cash moved, these can't appear in the three cash sections — but they still significantly affect financial position, so they must be disclosed.

07 · Preparing the Statement: The Six-Step Process

Topic 5.3

If detailed cash transaction records are available (easy path — no accrual assumptions needed):

1
Identify all cash inflows & outflows
2
Separate into Operating / Investing / Financing
3
Prepare the formal statement

If detailed cash records are NOT available, infer cash flows from how the balance sheet and income statement articulate — the full six-step process:

1
Compute change in cash balance
2
OPERATING: convert income statement accrual → cash, line by line
3
INVESTING: analyze changes in long-term assets
4
FINANCING: analyze changes in debt & equity
5
Reconcile to the computed cash change; prepare formal statement
6
Disclose non-cash items
Step 2 (Operating) — three adjustment types
1. Add back non-cash expenses (e.g., depreciation)
2. Eliminate gains/losses related to investing activities
3. Adjust for changes in operating (current) assets & liabilities
Book Value
Cost − Accumulated Depreciation
Used in Step 3 (investing analysis): comparing cash received from a sale to the asset's book value determines the gain or loss — which then must be eliminated from operating cash flow in Step 2.
General pattern for current asset/liability changes
Current asset goes down → Add (converted to cash, or cash was avoided)
Current asset goes up → Subtract (cash got tied up)
Current liability goes up → Add (you delayed paying, preserving cash)
Current liability goes down → Subtract (you used cash to pay it)
Memory rule: "Assets oppose, liabilities agree." Asset changes move opposite to cash; liability changes move the same direction as cash.

08 · Direct vs. Indirect Method

Topic 5.3 — two ways to REPORT the same operating cash flow number

Same answer, different presentation: both methods produce the identical operating cash flow figure and rest on the same underlying analysis. Only the operating section differs — investing and financing look the same either way.
Direct MethodIndirect Method
Starting pointSkips net income entirely — lists each operating cash flow line by line (cash collected from customers, cash paid for inventory, interest, taxes, etc.)Starts with net income, shows a series of adjustments, arrives at operating cash flow
How to identify itFirst line is a cash receipt/payment itemFirst number shown is net income — that's the giveaway
ReadabilityUnderstandable by anyone — no accounting training needed (the "mother test")Confusing to non-accountants — "why are we subtracting a gain?"
AdvantagesEasy to understand; allows line-item trend analysis over multiple yearsHighlights accrual assumptions; a genuine cash-flow management tool; easy to do after practice
Who uses itAlmost no oneAlmost everyone
Indirect method adjustment logic
Depreciation expense → Add back. It was subtracted computing net income, but no cash left the company — adding it back reverses a non-cash deduction (it does NOT mean depreciation creates cash).

Gain on sale of equipment → Subtract. The gain was added into net income, but that cash belongs in the investing section — subtracting it evicts investing traces from operating cash flow.
Why almost every company chooses indirect despite being harder to read: it puts the #1 most important number in accounting (net income) and the #2 (operating cash flow) on one page — with a reconciliation explaining every reason they differ. It also highlights management levers, e.g., don't aggressively pay down accounts payable if you need short-term cash — the indirect method makes that trade-off visible; the direct method doesn't.

09 · Forecasting: Pro Forma Statements

Topic 5.5

Pro forma cash flow statement
= A prediction of the future actual cash flow statement, built using the same six-step process on a projected balance sheet & income statement
Knowledge of how the three primary financial statements articulate allows one to forecast how interactions among management decisions might affect a company's future financial position.

A cash flow projection serves three audiences:

1

The Company

Plans ahead for timing of new loans, stock issuances, and long-term asset acquisitions.

2

Potential Lenders

Evaluate the likelihood that a loan will be repaid.

3

Potential Investors

Evaluate the likelihood of receiving cash dividends in the future.

10 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The mix-ups that show up most often

  • Notes receivable is NOT a cash equivalent — it's an IOU with credit risk and a longer maturity, even though it's a current asset.
  • Dividends received vs. dividends paid — received = operating (investment income). Paid = financing (return of capital to owners). This asymmetry is the single most-tested gotcha.
  • Interest paid AND interest received are both operating under U.S. GAAP, despite sounding like financing/investing items.
  • One loan payment, two classifications — the principal portion is financing; the interest portion is operating, even though both leave the bank account in the same transaction.
  • Whose stock? Buying another company's stock = investing. Buying back your OWN stock (treasury stock) = financing.
  • Trading vs. nontrading securities — trading securities (routine activity) stay in operating; nontrading (investment) securities go to investing.
  • Direct and indirect methods produce the SAME operating cash flow number — they differ only in presentation, not calculation.
  • Adding back depreciation does not mean it creates cash — it only reverses a non-cash subtraction that was made when computing net income.
  • Big Loss Scenario ≠ Rapid Growth Scenario — Big Loss is a NON-CASH writedown that hurts net income while cash stays healthy. Rapid Growth is a cash-consuming expansion that keeps net income looking strong while cash flow turns negative. Don't mix up which one is "too pessimistic" vs. "too optimistic."
  • Current asset vs. current liability changes move opposite ways relative to cash — assets oppose, liabilities agree. A decrease in AR is added; a decrease in AP is subtracted.
  • Normal healthy pattern: Operating positive, Investing negative, Financing depends on growth stage (positive = young/growing, negative = mature).
  • Non-cash investing/financing transactions still require disclosure even though no cash moved and they never appear in the three main sections.
