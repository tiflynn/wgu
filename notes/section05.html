01 · The Five Measures of Income

Topic 4.1 — each measure is really the same subtraction, done in stages

The income statement isn't one calculation — it's five checkpoints, each answering a slightly different question about performance. Zoomed all the way out, Net Income is still just Revenue − Expenses; the chain below is that same subtraction paused at meaningful stops along the way.

Quick summary:

 Sales Revenue
−Cost of Goods Sold
=Gross Profit
−Other Operating Expenses, Gains, and Losses
=Operating Income (EBIT)
−Interest Expense
+/−Miscellaneous revenues, expenses, gains, losses
=Income Before Taxes
−Income Tax Expense
=Income from Continuing Operations
+/−Income from Discontinued Operations
+/−Extraordinary Items
=Net Income
+/−Unrealized gains and losses not included in Net Income (OCI)
=Comprehensive Income
Placement note: Interest Expense, misc. items, and Income Tax Expense can be ordered/placed however a company chooses — this is just the prototypical layout. Everything from Discontinued Operations onward is reported net of taxes, so no separate tax line appears down there.
1 · Gross Profit
Sales − Cost of Goods and Services
The difference between the selling price of the product and the cost of the product. The "first cut" — profit from the core act of buying/making and selling, before any overhead. Manufacturer (e.g., GM): selling price − cost to manufacture (raw materials + labor + overhead — a complex number to compute). Merchandiser (e.g., Walmart): selling price − cost to purchase — much simpler, just "bought for this, sold for that."
Gross profit is the floor: if gross profit isn't positive, nothing further down the income statement can rescue the company — there's no magic way to generate net income if you're not even making money on the core product itself. This is why a company's very first focus has to be here.
2 · Operating Income a.k.a. EBIT
Gross Profit − Operating Expenses
Measures the performance of a company's fundamental business operations. Rule of thumb: every expense is operating except interest expense and income tax expense — those two are the only ones excused from this line.
The operating income "pie": think of operating income as a pie divided among three groups, in order of claim: 1) Lenders get their slice first — interest expense. 2) The government gets its slice next — income tax expense. 3) Owners get whatever's left — either paid out as dividends or kept in the business as retained earnings. This is exactly why interest and taxes are excluded from operating expenses: they're not costs of running the business, they're claims on the profit the business already generated.
3 · Income from Continuing Operations
Operating Income −/+ Interest Expense, misc. items − Income Tax Expense
Reflects the aspects of performance expected to continue into the future — which is exactly why it's considered the best baseline for forecasting next year's income. Placement note: companies have full freedom in where miscellaneous revenues/expenses/gains/losses go — above or below operating income. There's no fixed rule; the order shown here is just one common, prototypical way to lay it out.
4 · Net Income "The Bottom Line"
Income from Continuing Ops +/− Discontinued Ops +/− Extraordinary Items
The accountant's attempt to summarize a company's overall economic performance in one number for the period.
5 · Comprehensive Income
Net Income +/− Other Comprehensive Income (OCI)
An overall measure of the change in a company's wealth during the period, excluding new investment or investment withdrawal. OCI = unrealized, market-related gains/losses — value that moved on paper but hasn't been "cashed in" through an actual transaction, so it never touches Net Income. Common examples: foreign subsidiary translation (exchange rates shift), available-for-sale securities (portfolio value fluctuates), and hedging derivatives — all market-driven and outside the company's control. The FASB and IASB specifically instruct companies to prominently highlight comprehensive income, since it comes closest to an economist's concept of income: the total change in wealth.
Best number for forecasting: Income from Continuing Operations — it strips out the one-time noise (discontinued ops, extraordinary items) that tells you nothing about next year.

02 · Two Conceptual Views of Income

Topic 4.1 — two different lenses for "did the company actually get richer?"

Financial Capital Maintenance Physical Capital Maintenance
Tracks The dollar amount of equity (wealth) Actual physical resources & productive capacity
Ignores What form the wealth takes Dollar price tags / inflation effects
Both exclude New investment or investment withdrawal by owners — neither view counts owners putting money in or taking it out as "income"
Inflation example: a company starts the year with a machine worth $100,000; by year-end, an identical replacement costs $130,000. Financial capital maintenance says equity rose in dollar terms → that's income. Physical capital maintenance says: you'd need that extra $30,000 just to replace what you already had — you haven't gained any real productive capacity, so that's not real income, just inflation.

A worked example — there isn't just one right answer, there are (at least) three:

The House Example
Buy a house Jan 1 for $200,000 (your primary residence). Sell it Dec 31 for $220,000. General price inflation during the year: 3%. How much income did you have?
View Logic Income
Financial capital maintenance (simple) Just the cash difference: $220,000 − $200,000 $20,000
Inflation-adjusted financial $200,000 × 3% = $6,000 was only "keeping pace" with inflation, not a real gain — so only $20,000 − $6,000 counts $14,000
Physical capital maintenance Since this is your primary residence, you have to buy a replacement — and a similar house now costs about $220,000. You're no better housed than before. $0
The takeaway: "income" isn't a single obvious number — it depends entirely on which lens you're using. This is exactly why, whenever someone says "income" in an accounting context, the first question has to be: income by which measure?

Memory anchor: Financial = Figures (dollars). Physical = Production (machines/capacity).

03 · Revenue Items

Topic 4.2

In transaction analysis, Revenue = the amount of assets generated or liabilities satisfied through doing business. Revenue isn't the asset itself — it's the source of an asset (as opposed to borrowing or owner investment).

Revenue type Earned from
Sales Revenue The aggregate selling price of goods sold (retailers/manufacturers)
Service (fee) Revenue Services rendered, not goods sold — e.g., a plumber, a law firm, a CPA
Interest Revenue Money earned from lending funds to others
Other Revenue Licensing revenue, Franchise revenue, Rent revenue, Premium revenue

04 · Expense Items

Topic 4.2 — grouped the way the transcripts present them

In transaction analysis, Expense = the amount of assets consumed or liabilities created through doing business.

Cost of Goods Sold — its own category, not SG&A:

Expense What it covers
Cost of Goods Sold The cost recorded when a business sells goods to customers — only counts goods actually sold, not produced/purchased. Direct-matched to the sale, unlike everything below.

SG&A (Selling, General & Administrative) — the catch-all for costs of running the business that aren't COGS:

Expense What it covers
R&D Expensed in full in the U.S. since 1974 (FASB ruling) — a possible (not probable) future benefit doesn't qualify as an asset. Not the same treatment worldwide.
Wages & Salaries Manufacturer: folded into COGS instead, not SG&A. Service/retail: a separate SG&A line. Either way, "wages" is bigger than base pay — it bundles healthcare, payroll taxes, pensions, and stock-based comp too.
Bad Debt Expense The estimated amount of credit extended to customers that will never be collected
Depreciation Expense Systematic allocation of a long-term asset's acquisition cost over its service period. Land doesn't depreciate. Non-production assets (office, retail) — SG&A. Production-line depreciation folds into COGS instead, same logic as manufacturer wages.
SG&A is a real grab-bag — the transcripts cite actual filings: Con Edison's $21.3M estimated nuclear waste decontamination cost (2000), Home Depot's $86M pre-opening costs (2004), Altria's $3.53B litigation settlement charge (2003), and Sears' $475M reaffirmation charge (1997). Different every year, different for every company — that's why it's a catch-all.

Not operating expenses at all — sit below Operating Income, per the rule of thumb:

Expense What it covers
Interest Expense Cost of borrowing — a financing decision, not part of core operations. Tax-deductible.
Income Tax Expense The total estimated amount of tax expected to eventually be paid, based on current-year reported income
Manufacturer vs. service/retail wages: a factory worker's wages get baked right into the cost of the product. A retail cashier's wages get their own separate SG&A line instead.
Optimal bad debt expense isn't zero. Zero bad debt means you're turning away customers who were creditworthy enough to pay — the skill in selling on credit is picking the right risk tolerance, not avoiding bad debt entirely.
Why net income ≠ taxable income: companies keep three sets of books — financial reporting (GAAP, for lenders/investors), income tax books (IRS rules), and internal management books. Different purposes, different rules, different numbers. Income Tax Expense estimates what's eventually owed under set #2, reported alongside set #1.

05 · Gains, Losses & the "Big Bath"

Topic 4.2 — activities peripheral to a company's main operations

The rule: normal business or not?
Revenues/expenses = primary items (assets created/consumed doing what the company normally does). Gains/losses = peripheral items (money made/lost outside normal operations). It's not about the transaction type — it's about whether that transaction is this company's business. Example: Ford sells trucks for a living, so selling a truck is revenue/expense for Ford. Walmart sells an old delivery truck it no longer needs — same transaction, but it's a gain/loss for Walmart, since selling trucks isn't what Walmart does.
Item Definition
Impairment Loss Recognized when the recorded amount of an asset is reduced because it has declined in value
Restructuring Charge Covers asset write-downs and employee termination/relocation costs from reorganizing operations
The "big bath" strategy: because management has discretion over the amount and timing of restructuring charges, some companies dump extra bad news into an already-bad year so future years look better by comparison. The SEC has flagged this as a potential form of earnings manipulation.

06 · Below-the-Line: Discontinued Ops & Extraordinary Items

Topic 4.2 — everything below Income from Continuing Operations is reported net of taxes

Income (Loss) from Discontinued Operations
The net income or loss from a business segment a company has decided to dispose of, once the disposal qualifies as "discontinued." Includes both the segment's operating results and any gain/loss on disposal, combined into one net summary line — the segment's total revenue is disclosed separately in the footnotes.
The one-year test: to qualify as a discontinued operation, disposal must be expected to be complete within one year of the end of the reporting period — not one year from the decision date. Example: a decision made Feb 1, 2021 falls in FY2021 (ends Dec 31, 2021); the clock runs to Dec 31, 2022. While pending, the segment's assets/liabilities are listed separately on the balance sheet.
Net of taxes, worked: a $100 extraordinary gain taxed at 40% shows as one line: $60. The tax effect is embedded right in the number — nothing else to add or subtract.
Extraordinary Items
Must be both: (1) Unusual in nature — not the kind of thing that happens in this company's normal business, and (2) Infrequent in occurrence — not reasonably expected to happen again. Real example: ExxonMobil, 2000 — forced to divest pipeline assets on antitrust grounds during the Exxon-Mobil merger, resulting in a $1.7 billion extraordinary gain.
Textbook note: real-world current GAAP eliminated extraordinary items as a separate category in 2015 — but this textbook still teaches the older framework, so keep it in the Net Income formula for exam purposes. It's also a U.S.-only concept; internationally, there is no extraordinary items category.

08 · Revenue Recognition & Matching

Topic 4.4

Revenue Recognition
Revenue is recognized when the work is substantially complete and the customer has paid or promised to pay — typically at the point of sale, regardless of when cash actually arrives.
The Matching Concept
An expense should be recognized in the same period as the revenue it helped generate — pairs the cost with the payoff it helped create, like matching socks to their pair.

Three methods used to decide when to recognize an expense:

1

Direct Matching

A clear, one-to-one link to a specific sale. Example: Cost of Goods Sold — you know exactly what the item you sold cost you.

2

Systematic Allocation

Benefits a measurable stretch of future time, so cost is spread on a schedule. Example: Depreciation — also applies to prepaid items like insurance or rent.

3

Immediate Recognition

No reliable way to trace the cost to any specific future revenue, so it's expensed the moment it's incurred. Example: Advertising.

Quick test: "Can I trace this to one specific sale?" → Direct Matching. "Does it benefit a measurable stretch of future time?" → Systematic Allocation. "Is there no reliable link to future revenue at all?" → Immediate Recognition.

09 · Transaction Analysis & Forecasting

Topics 4.5 & 4.6

Retained Earnings
Net Income − Dividends
Revenues increase Retained Earnings; expenses and dividends both decrease it.
Expanded Accounting Equation
Assets = Liabilities + Paid-in Capital + (Revenues − Expenses − Dividends)
Equity splits into what owners put in (Paid-in Capital) and what the business earned and kept (the Revenues/Expenses/Dividends piece feeding Retained Earnings).
Pro-forma Financial Statements
A prediction of what future financial statements will look like if planned operating, investing, and financing activities are implemented. Also called budgeted, forecasted, or projected financial statements. Forecasting typically starts with a sales forecast, which sets the expected scale of future operations.

What drives each item, for forecasting purposes:

Item Scales with
Cash, Accounts Receivable, Inventory, Accounts Payable Sales level — increase naturally as sales increase
Cost of Goods Sold Sales level — fairly constant % of sales
Property, Plant & Equipment Long-term strategic plans, not current sales (excess capacity absorbs growth)
Depreciation Expense The amount of PP&E on the books
Interest Expense The balance in interest-bearing debt
Income Tax Expense A fairly constant % of income before taxes
Long-term debt, Paid-in Capital Management's financing decisions, not sales

10 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The mix-ups that show up most often

  • Dividends are never an expense — they reduce Retained Earnings, not Net Income. If a problem hands you dividends alongside sales/net income, it's almost always a distractor: Expenses = Sales − Net Income, ignore dividends entirely.
  • Distractor lists in Net Income problems — accounts payable, capital stock, and beginning retained earnings are recurring distractors. Net Income = Revenue − Expenses only.
  • Extraordinary items are textbook-specific — this course keeps the pre-2015 GAAP treatment (its own line in Net Income) even though current real-world GAAP eliminated the category in 2015. Also a U.S.-only concept internationally.
  • Extraordinary items need BOTH conditions — unusual in nature AND infrequent in occurrence. Missing either one disqualifies it.
  • The one-year discontinued-ops test runs from the end of the reporting period, not from the decision date.
  • Accounts Payable scales with a 40% sales increase — PP&E doesn't (excess capacity, strategic/lumpy), bank loans don't (a financing choice), and operating profit doesn't move 1:1 (fixed costs create operating leverage).
  • Manufacturer wages vs. service/retail wages — a manufacturer's production wages get folded into COGS; a service/retail business reports wages as a separate expense line.
  • R&D is always expensed in the U.S. because the future benefit is only possible, not probable — but this treatment isn't universal worldwide.
  • Advertising vs. insurance/rent — advertising is Immediate Recognition (no reliable link to specific future revenue), while a prepaid expense like insurance or rent is Systematic Allocation (a measurable stretch of future benefit) — easy to mix up since both "feel" future-oriented.
  • Financial vs. Physical capital maintenance — Financial tracks dollar-denominated equity; Physical tracks real productive capacity. They can disagree, especially with inflation (or deflation) in asset replacement costs.
  • Single-step vs. multi-step — single-step groups revenue/expenses into one subtraction; multi-step is the one that emphasizes gross profit and operating income as separate subtotals. Don't swap these two definitions.
  • Gain/loss vs. revenue/expense depends on the company, not the transaction — selling a truck is revenue for Ford (that's their business) but a gain/loss for Walmart (it isn't). Same transaction, different classification.
  • Bad debt expense of $0 is a red flag, not a good sign — it means the company is too conservative in extending credit, not that it's doing well.
