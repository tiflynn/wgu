01 · Balance Sheet Order & Liquidity

02 · Current Assets

Topic 3.1 — assets expected to be used, sold, or replaced within one year

"Current" doesn't mean the asset disappears — it means it gets used, sold, or replaced within the year. You'll still have cash a year from now, just not that $100 bill; a grocery store will still have milk inventory, just not that carton. Current assets are constantly cycling in and out of the business.

CategoryDescription
Cash ★ Big 3Coins, currency, and bank accounts — plus cash equivalents: CDs, U.S. Treasury securities, and other short-term interest-bearing securities maturing in 3 months or less. Reported together on the balance sheet as "cash and cash equivalents."
Accounts receivable ★ Big 3Standard credit arrangement, typically collected in 30–60 days
Inventory ★ Big 3Goods held for resale in the normal course of business
Prepaid expensesExpenses paid in advance, before the related benefit is received
Investment securitiesShort-term stocks and bonds; bonds typically mature in 3–12 months
The "Big 3" current assets: if you're ever asked to list current assets from memory, start with cash, accounts receivable, and inventory — these are the most common current assets and form the core of a business's day-to-day operations (see the operating cycle below). Prepaid expenses and investment securities round out the full list of five.
Factoring accounts receivable: a company that needs cash now, rather than waiting the usual 30–60 days to collect, can sell its accounts receivable to a financial institution in exchange for immediate cash — this is called factoring. It's a good illustration of why AR is a genuine asset: it's valuable enough that someone else will pay real cash for it today, before the customer has even paid.

The Big 3 aren't just common — they're the three assets a business cycles through repeatedly to generate cash:

The Operating Cycle: a business starts with cash, uses it to buy inventory, sells that inventory (often on credit, creating accounts receivable), then collects the cash owed — ideally ending up with more cash than it started with. That surplus gets reinvested, and the cycle repeats. This is exactly why cash, inventory, and accounts receivable are grouped together as the Big 3 current assets: they're not "current" just by definition — they're actively recycling through this loop within the year, and the whole point of running the cycle is to generate a cash surplus each time through.
Trading vs. available-for-sale securities: both are reported at current market value, but unrealized ("paper") gains/losses land differently — trading securities flow through the income statement, while available-for-sale securities go directly to equity (AOCI), bypassing net income. More on AOCI in section 06.

03 · Long-Term Assets & Intangibles

Topic 3.1 — assets still expected to be on hand at the next balance sheet date

CategoryIncludes
Long-term investmentsStocks and bonds held for the long term
Property, Plant & Equipment (PPE)Land, buildings, machinery, tools, furniture, vehicles
Intangible assetsNo physical characteristics: trademarks, brand names, copyrights, franchises, goodwill
Other long-term assetsDeferred income tax asset; net pension asset (when the pension fund is overfunded)
Exam trap — intangibles are only recorded if purchased: a company's home-grown brand reputation, customer loyalty, or internally developed technology is never recorded, no matter how valuable — there's no reliable, objective transaction price. An intangible asset only makes it onto the balance sheet when it's purchased from another party (a bought patent, trademark, or copyright) or arises as goodwill from acquiring an entire company for more than the fair value of its identifiable net assets.
What goodwill actually represents: think of goodwill as the economic value of the relationships a company has built up over time — with customers, suppliers, banks, employees, and government officials. Those relationships make a business run better, but they're hard to put a reliable number on until an acquisition actually happens and a real price gets paid for the whole company.
Companies don't actually want deferred tax assets or overfunded pension assets: it sounds backwards, but neither is a "win." A deferred tax asset means a tax benefit the company would rather have now is instead delayed to a future year. An overfunded pension plan means cash is tied up protecting employee pensions instead of being available to buy buildings, equipment, or inventory. Both are real assets — just not ones a company is eager to hold.

04 · Current Liabilities

Topic 3.1 — obligations expected to be paid or satisfied within one year

CategoryDescription
Accounts payableThe flip side of accounts receivable — the most common current liability
Accrued liabilitiesBuilt up gradually: wages payable, taxes payable, utilities payable
Short-term loans payableA formal, interest-bearing loan due within one year
Current portion of long-term debtThe slice of a mortgage or other long-term liability due within the next 12 months
Unearned revenueAn obligation to provide a service to a customer who has already paid but not yet received it
Exam trap — unearned revenue is NOT revenue: despite the name, unearned revenue is a liability. Cash came in first, but the work/service is still owed — it's a debt in the form of future service, not income.

05 · Long-Term Liabilities

Topic 3.1 — obligations not expected to be paid within one year

CategoryDescription
Long-term debtLong-term loans, notes, bonds, and mortgages
Capital lease obligationsA lease structured so it's economically the same as a debt-financed purchase
Deferred income tax liabilityTax expected to be paid in future years on income already reported in the income statement but not yet taxed
Other long-term liabilitiesEmployee retirement/pension benefit obligations
Loan vs. note — what's the difference? A "loan" typically implies borrowing from a bank or financial institution. A "note" is more general — it's just a written promise to repay, and you could write one to borrow from anyone (another company, an individual investor). Economically they function the same way; the terminology just signals who's usually on the other side of the transaction.
Bonds are tradable: unlike a typical bank loan, the original bondholder isn't stuck holding the bond until maturity. They can sell it to someone else, who can sell it to someone else again — the bond changes hands while the company keeps making the same interest and principal payments to whoever holds it at the time. This tradability is a key feature that sets bonds apart from a standard note or loan.

Topic 3.1

Retained Earnings
Beginning RE + Net Income − Net Losses − Dividends = Ending RE
A common mistake is thinking retained earnings is only reduced by dividends. It's reduced by both net losses and dividend payments, and increased by net income.
Treasury stock = contra-equity: shares a company repurchases from its own stockholders are subtracted from total stockholders' equity — a negative equity line, not a liability. Like a dividend, it distributes cash to stockholders, but only to those who sell their shares back and give up ownership; a dividend goes to everyone while all stockholders keep their shares.

Accumulated Other Comprehensive Income (AOCI) groups equity changes from market/exchange-rate movements, kept separate from net income:

1

Foreign currency translation

Change in a foreign subsidiary's equity (in dollar terms) as exchange rates move — applies only to companies with foreign subsidiaries.

2

Unrealized gains/losses — AFS securities

Available-for-sale securities' "paper" value swings go to equity/AOCI, not the income statement (contrast with trading securities).

3

Unrealized gains/losses — derivatives

Some unrealized gains/losses from options, futures, and similar instruments are also reported as part of AOCI.

07 · Non-U.S. Format & Working Capital

Topic 3.2

Working Capital
Current Assets − Current Liabilities
Also called net current assets.
Non-U.S. balance sheets: frequently list property, plant, and equipment first — before current assets — reversing the U.S. liquidity-first order. They also often group current assets and current liabilities together and label the difference as working capital, right on the face of the statement.

08 · Recognition, Valuation & Disclosure

Topic 3.3

TermWhat it answers
RecognitionShould this item be included in the financial statements at all, boiled down into one number?
ValuationOnce recognized, what dollar amount should be assigned to it?
DisclosureThe alternative to recognition — describing details in a narrative note, without a number in the statements themselves
Recognition needs BOTH conditions met: an item must satisfy the appropriate definition (asset/liability) and be measurable in a reliable way. "Probable" alone isn't enough — a lawsuit that's probable to be lost but has no reliably estimable dollar amount doesn't get recognized as a liability; it gets disclosed in the notes instead.
Reliability vs. relevance: historical cost is very reliable (an actual, verifiable transaction) but can lack relevance over time as market conditions change. Market value is more relevant but not always reliable. This is why an asset's market value going up does not get recognized — but a significant, permanent decline in value can trigger an impairment write-down (conservatism: recognize losses promptly, defer gains until realized).

The five-step path from a real-world event to a finished financial statement:

1
Creation
Events create economic assets and liabilities
2
Recognition
Decision to include an item in the financial statements
3
Valuation
Measurement of the dollar amount to record
4
Integration
Total impact on the financial statements is determined
5
Preparation
The financial statements themselves are prepared

09 · Transaction Analysis & Executory Contracts

Topic 3.4

Transaction analysis is the process of determining how an economic event impacts the financial statements.

Executory contracts are NOT recognized — not even at $0: an executory contract is an exchange of promises about the future (e.g., a signed employment contract, a future purchase agreement). Since neither party has performed yet, nothing appears on the balance sheet — not as a $0 placeholder line, just no mention at all. It may be disclosed in the notes if material.

Recognition is triggered once one side begins performing — example: a $1,000/week employee, paid Fridays.

Point in timeWages payable
Contract signed (no work yet)$0 — nothing recorded, purely executory
End of day 1 worked$200
End of day 4 worked$800
Day 5 (payday) — work done, then paidLiability hits $1,000, then cash pays it off → $0
In practice: companies don't literally post a journal entry every single day. Payroll software tracks accrual in the background, and the wages payable liability is formally recorded at payroll run dates and period-end closing dates (via adjusting entries) — the daily walkthrough is a teaching model for the underlying accrual logic, not literal daily bookkeeping.

10 · Asset & Financing Mix, Matching Maturities

Topic 3.5

Matching Maturities
Short-term assets ↔ Short-term debt  |  Long-term assets ↔ Long-term debt
Financing should be timed to match how long it takes the related asset to generate cash. Mismatching creates liquidity risk — being forced to repay or refinance debt before the asset has generated enough cash.
Example of a mismatch: financing a building (a 20+ year asset) with a 90-day loan. The building can't generate enough cash in 90 days to repay the loan, forcing a risky refinance — exactly the liquidity risk matching maturities is meant to avoid.

11 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The mix-ups that show up most often

  • Assets ≠ sources of financing. Assets are the resources acquired; liabilities and owners' equity together are the sources of financing (evidence of how those resources were acquired).
  • Retained earnings is reduced by BOTH net losses and dividends — not dividends alone.
  • Unearned revenue is a liability, not revenue — despite the name, it's an obligation to deliver a service already paid for.
  • Treasury stock is a contra-equity account (a subtraction from equity), not a liability.
  • Intangible assets are only recorded if purchased — home-grown brand value, customer loyalty, or internally developed tech never make the balance sheet; goodwill only arises from acquiring another company. Goodwill itself represents the value of built-up relationships (customers, suppliers, banks, employees, government) — hard to price until a real acquisition happens.
  • Deferred tax assets and overfunded pension assets are NOT things companies want — despite being assets, both mean a benefit (tax savings, freed-up cash) is delayed or tied up rather than available now.
  • Bonds are tradable; a standard loan or note usually isn't — the original bondholder can resell to someone else, who keeps collecting the interest and principal payments in their place.
  • Recognition requires the definition AND reliable measurement — "probable" isn't enough on its own (the unresolved-lawsuit example).
  • Executory contracts are not recognized at all — not even at $0. A signature alone isn't a transaction; only actual performance triggers an entry.
  • Historical cost doesn't move up for gains, but CAN move down for impairment — assets aren't written up to market value, but a significant, permanent decline can trigger a write-down (conservatism in action).
  • Non-U.S. balance sheets often flip the order — PP&E first, current assets/liabilities grouped together as working capital.
  • Matching maturities: short-term assets funded with short-term debt, long-term assets with long-term debt — mismatching creates liquidity risk.
