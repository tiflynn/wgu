01 · What Is Accounting?

Core definitions

Accounting — the two-part definition
Record daily activity  +  Report it in summary form
Accounting is recording the day-to-day financial activities of a company AND organizing that information into summary reports used to evaluate the company's financial status. Both halves are required — recording without reporting is just bookkeeping; reporting without recording is impossible.
TermDefinition
BookkeepingThe preservation of a systematic, quantitative record of an activity. Without it, good business is impossible.
Accounting SystemHandles routine bookkeeping tasks AND structures the information so it can be used to evaluate performance and financial status.
Forward-looking purpose: accounting information is intended to be useful in making decisions about the future — even though it records the past, the whole point is to inform what happens next.
Rules + judgment: the practice of accounting involves adherence to established rules as well as the use of judgment. It is not purely mechanical — many situations require professional interpretation.

02 · The Three Financial Statements

Financial accounting's three primary outputs

Balance Sheet Snapshot at a point in time
Assets = Liabilities + Owners' Equity
Reports what a company owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and what belongs to the owners. The two sides must always balance.
Income Statement Over a period of time
Net Income = Revenues − Expenses
Reports the amount of net income earned during a period. If revenues exceed expenses, the company has a profit; if expenses exceed revenues, it has a loss.
Statement of Cash Flows Over a period of time
Operating
→
Investing
→
Financing
Reports cash collected and paid out across these three activity types. Together, all three cover where a company's cash came from and where it went.
Snapshot vs. period: the Balance Sheet is a single point-in-time snapshot. The Income Statement and Statement of Cash Flows both cover a period. Don't mix these up when a question asks which statement to use.

04 · GAAP & Accounting Rules

The rulebook behind consistent reporting

GAAP Generally Accepted Accounting Principles
Origins: early 1900s  |  Set by: FASB
Ensures consistency and transparency in financial reporting, so companies can be fairly compared against one another.
Memory tip: think of a gap between two companies' data — GAAP fills that gap by making every company report the same way, so comparisons are fair.

05 · Accounting Organizations

Who writes the rules, who enforces them, and who's separate entirely

OrgRole
FASB (Financial Accounting Standards Board)Private body (NOT government) — supported by the U.S. business community, financial analysts, and practicing accountants. Sets U.S. accounting standards (GAAP).
SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission)Regulates U.S. stock exchanges. Ensures a fair information environment so investors aren't misled by hidden or manipulated data.
AICPA (American Institute of CPAs)Professional organization of CPAs. A CPA = college accounting classes + passing the CPA exam + state requirements. A CPA firm gives freelance accounting advice.
PCAOB (Public Company Accounting Oversight Board)Inspects audit practices of registered firms. Can bar a firm from auditing SEC-registered (public) companies.
IRS (Internal Revenue Service)Establishes when income is taxed. NO role in GAAP. Financial statements are NOT used to calculate tax owed.
IASB (International Accounting Standards Board)Formed to develop worldwide accounting standards. Increasingly accepted globally, but FASB still governs the U.S.
Quick reference: FASB = writes U.S. rules  |  SEC = stock market cop  |  AICPA = accountants' club  |  PCAOB = audit police  |  IRS = tax only (separate)  |  IASB = international

07 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The mix-ups that show up most often

  • FASB is private, not government. It's supported by the business community, financial analysts, and practicing accountants — not a government agency.
  • Financial statements ≠ tax returns. The IRS does NOT use a company's financial statements to determine how much tax it owes — these are two separate systems.
  • IASB ≠ the U.S. standard. IASB standards are increasingly accepted worldwide, but FASB/GAAP still governs the United States.
  • PCAOB inspects auditors, not companies directly. It has the power to bar an audit FIRM from auditing SEC-registered companies.
  • Balance Sheet = snapshot. Income Statement and Statement of Cash Flows = over a period of time. Don't mix up "point in time" vs. "period."
  • SOX increases scrutiny of production of financial statements — not just their content. It targets how statements are made, not only what they say.
