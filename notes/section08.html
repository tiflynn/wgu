01 · Why Sell on Credit? Costs & Credit Card Companies

Topic 7.1

The trade-off
Benefit: More sales  |  Cost: Bad debts + Bookkeeping + Carrying costs
Selling on credit lets a business capture sales it would otherwise lose — the customer who wants to buy but doesn't have cash on hand today. That benefit has to be weighed against three real costs.

The three costs of selling on credit:

1

Bad Debts

Some customers who buy on credit simply will not pay. It's an unavoidable cost of extending credit at all.

2

Bookkeeping Costs

Developing and maintaining a system to track sales, evaluate creditworthiness, and bill customers. A cash-only roadside stand has none of this.

3

Carrying Costs

Money tied up in Accounts Receivable is money not available to use elsewhere — the company must finance operations while it waits to be paid.

Credit card companies as outsourcers: Visa, MasterCard, Amex, and Discover let a business accept "credit" sales without bearing any of the three costs directly. The business gets cash immediately; the card company waits to collect from the customer and absorbs the bad-debt, bookkeeping, and carrying-cost risk itself — in exchange for a transaction fee (e.g., 0.5%–2% of the sale).

02 · Credit Terms & Sales Discounts

Topic 7.1

Reading the code
[Discount %] / [Discount days] , net [Final due date]
Example: 2/10, net 30 — read "two-ten, net thirty" — means pay within 10 days for a 2% discount, or pay the full amount within 30 days. EOM means "end of month" and replaces a fixed day count with the calendar's end-of-month date.
TermsDiscountDiscount windowFinal due date
2/10, net 302%Within 10 days30 days
4/15, net 604%Within 15 days60 days
2/10, net EOM2%Within 10 daysEnd of month
Skipping the discount is an expensive "loan": on a $1,000 purchase with 2/10, net 30 terms, paying on day 10 costs $980; waiting until day 30 costs $1,000. That extra $20 is effectively interest on a 20-day loan of $980 (2.04%) — and 20-day periods repeat about 18.25 times a year, so it annualizes to roughly 37.2%. Well-organized businesses take the discount almost every time.
Sales discounts on the books
Gross Sales − Sales Discounts (contra account) = Net Sales
A sales discount is a contra account — a subtraction from Gross Sales. Externally, published financial statements only show Net Sales; the gross price and the discount detail behind it stay internal.

03 · Budgeting Cash Receipts

Topic 7.1

A given month's cash receipts can be pieced together from up to four sources, depending on how many months the collection pattern spans:

Current-month
cash sales
+
Current-month
credit, collected now
+
Prior-month
credit, collected now
+
2-months-prior
credit, collected now
Worked example
Jan sales $80,000 · Feb sales $100,000 · 30% same month / 70% month after
February collections = (Jan sales × 70%) + (Feb sales × 30%) = $56,000 + $30,000 = $86,000. Each month's collections draw on that month's own sales plus the "leftover" tail from prior months' sales.

04 · The Beginning-A/R "Cannot Be Determined" Trap

Topic 7.1 — high-value exam trap

The trap: if a problem gives you a beginning Accounts Receivable balance and an aging-based collection pattern (e.g., 40% same month / 50% month after / 10% second month after) — but does not break the beginning balance into its month-of-origin components — the correct answer for the very first forecasted month's collections is "cannot be determined."

Why: the beginning A/R balance is made up of unresolved sales from two or more prior months, each contributing to the next collection at a different rate (one month's remainder collects at the "month following" rate, another's at the "second month following" rate). Without knowing the origin-month split, you can't calculate how much of that lump sum converts to cash specifically in the next month.

Problem typeWhat's givenFirst-month answer
Beginning A/R + aging %, no origin breakdown Lump-sum beginning balance + a collection pattern by age Cannot be determined
Beginning & ending A/R roll-forward Beginning balance, ending balance, and credit sales — no aging % Fully computable: Beg. A/R + Credit Sales − End. A/R
Later months of an aging-pattern problem Only known monthly sales figures feed the calculation — no lump sum involved Fully computable — apply the % pattern directly
Rule of thumb: the ambiguity only exists for the first forecasted month, when a lump-sum beginning balance and an aging % pattern appear together. Every later month is fully computable because it only draws on explicitly stated monthly sales figures.

05 · From Sales to Purchases (Topic 7.2)

Topic 7.2 — Budgeting Cash Outflows

Sales-to-Purchases Conversion
Purchases = Sales × Cost of Goods Sold %
A company doesn't pay its supplier the retail selling price — it pays the wholesale purchase cost. If COGS runs 80% of sales and sales are forecasted at $100,000, purchases are $80,000.
Steady-state assumption: when there's no change in inventory levels month to month, a company purchases exactly what it sells (cost-wise) that same month — so forecasted Purchases = forecasted Cost of Goods Sold. This is what lets a single COGS% conversion double as the purchases figure.

06 · Budgeting Cash Disbursements

Topic 7.2

Just like receipts, disbursements for a given month draw on purchases from that month and prior months, per the payment pattern — but watch for whether a same-month percentage is included:

Pattern typeExampleWhat it means
No same-month payment 50% month following / 50% second month following Nothing is paid in the month purchased — payment starts the month after
Includes same-month payment 30% same month / 50% month following / 20% second month following The target month's own purchases contribute too — don't forget that third piece
Worked example (no same-month payment)
Nov purchases $60,000 · Dec purchases $90,000 · 50%/50% pattern
January payment = (Nov × 50%, second month following) + (Dec × 50%, month following) = $30,000 + $45,000 = $75,000.
Worked example (with same-month payment)
Jan $90,000 · Feb $70,000 · Mar $110,000 · 30% / 50% / 20% pattern
March payment = (Jan × 20%) + (Feb × 50%) + (Mar × 30%) = $18,000 + $35,000 + $33,000 = $86,000.

07 · Net Cash Position & the Borrowing Formula

Topic 7.2

Net Cash Flow
Net Cash Flow = Forecasted Receipts − Forecasted Disbursements
A positive result is a surplus; a negative result is a deficit — the signal that short-term financing may be needed that month.
Borrowing Needed
Borrowing = Desired Ending Balance − (Beginning Balance + Net Cash Flow)
Find where the company would land without borrowing (Beginning Balance + Net Cash Flow), then borrow exactly enough to close the gap to the desired ending balance. If the result is negative or zero, no borrowing is needed.
Cash Outflows from Total Expenses
Cash Outflows = Total Budgeted Expenses − Depreciation (and other non-cash expenses)
Depreciation is a non-cash expense — no check is written for it this period, since the related cash was already paid when the asset was purchased. Back it out (along with amortization, if present) to get from total expenses to actual cash outflows.
ScenarioNumbersBorrowing
First year of operation (beginning cash = $0) Collections $697,500 · Cash outflows $680,000 · Desired ending $30,000 $30,000 − ($0 + $17,500) = $12,500
With an existing cash cushion Beginning $50,000 · Receipts $500,000 · Disbursements $520,000 · Desired ending $40,000 $40,000 − ($50,000 − $20,000) = $10,000

08 · Why Budget in Advance

Topics 7.1 & 7.2 — the payoff

The Valentine's Day story: a business owner who shows up at the bank the day they desperately need cash — employees double-parked outside waiting to be paid — looks risky and pays a high interest rate. A business owner who arrives months earlier with a full cash budget, showing exactly when a shortfall will hit and when it will be repaid, looks organized and credible, and gets much better terms.

Once a forecasted deficit is visible (e.g., forecasted on January 1st for a February shortfall), a company on solid footing has several calm options:

1
Arrange a line of credit
Negotiated in advance, at better rates
2
Delay supplier payments
Push a payment back with advance notice
3
Encourage faster customer payment
Speed up incoming collections

09 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The mix-ups that show up most often

  • Increased sales is the BENEFIT, not a cost — the three costs of selling on credit are bad debts, bookkeeping, and carrying costs. Watch for "increased sales volume" appearing as a wrong-answer distractor among the costs.
  • Beginning A/R + aging % pattern, no origin breakdown → "cannot be determined" for the first forecasted month's collections. Don't default to "collect it all next month" here — that shortcut only applies to plain beginning/ending A/R roll-forward problems with no aging pattern.
  • "Net 30" is the outer deadline, not the discount window — in "2/10, net 30," the "10" is the discount window and the "30" is the final due date if no discount is taken.
  • Sales discounts are a contra account — subtracted from Gross Sales to arrive at Net Sales, which is the only figure reported externally.
  • Skipping a discount is expensive — the implicit annualized interest rate of missing a typical discount (like 2/10, net 30) is roughly 37%, far worse than most loans.
  • Purchases = Sales × COGS%, and under the no-inventory-change assumption, Purchases = Cost of Goods Sold. Don't accidentally use the sales figure itself when the question asks for purchases/payments.
  • Check for a same-month payment percentage in the disbursement pattern — some patterns start payment the month after purchase (nothing paid the same month), others include a same-month portion. Missing this changes which months feed into your target month.
  • Depreciation is subtracted from total expenses to get cash outflows — it's a non-cash expense, since the related cash was already paid when the asset was purchased.
  • Borrowing formula always starts from where you'd land without borrowing — Beginning Balance + Net Cash Flow — then makes up the difference to the desired ending balance. First-year companies start beginning cash at $0.
  • Planning ahead gets better loan terms — a company that forecasts a shortfall weeks in advance looks credible to a lender; one that discovers it the day it happens looks risky and pays more.
