01 · Why C-V-P Analysis Matters

Topic 11.1

Cost-Volume-Profit (C-V-P) Analysis
How does profit change as sales volume, price, or costs change?
C-V-P is a Planning tool — every C-V-P question is asked before a decision is made ("should I open this business?", "should I raise prices?"), never used to track results after the fact (that's Controlling).

What each C-V-P variable depends on:

VariableDepends on
Sales RevenueNumber of units sold · Selling price per unit · Mix of items sold
Variable CostNumber of units sold · Variable cost per unit · Mix of items sold
Fixed CostManagement's choice of the cost level — does NOT depend on the number of units sold
ProfitSales Revenue − Variable Cost − Fixed Cost
REMEMBER THIS (verbatim): Key factors involved in C-V-P analysis are:
  • Revenues from the sales prices charged for goods and services
  • Fixed and variable costs
  • Sales volume
  • Mix of products
  • Resulting profits

Break-even analysis is one specific type of C-V-P analysis: finding the exact sales volume where profit equals $0. The classic illustration is a charity banquet — a $250 ticket price, a $40 per-person caterer cost (variable), and an $18,000 raffle car (fixed). Contribution margin per ticket = $250 − $40 = $210, so break-even = $18,000 ÷ $210 ≈ 86 attendees. The value of running this calculation before committing is that it counteracts the natural over-optimism of anyone launching a new venture — a reality check grounded in numbers rather than gut feeling.

02 · Basic Cost Behavior Patterns

Topic 11.2

Cost typeTotal behaviorPer-unit behaviorExample
VariableChanges with volumeConstantMeat cost per hamburger ($0.50, always)
FixedConstantChanges inversely with volumeRestaurant rent ($2,300/month, regardless of sales)
SteppedConstant within a step, jumps at thresholdsConstant within a stepOne supervisor per 2,000 gallons of ice cream produced
MixedChanges with volume, but with a floorDecreases toward the variable rate as volume growsMall rent: $4,000 base + 1% of sales
REMEMBER THIS (verbatim):
  • The two basic cost behavior patterns are: variable → variable in total, fixed per unit; fixed → fixed in total, variable per unit
  • Stepped costs increase with the level of activity but in steps instead of smoothly
  • Mixed costs have both a fixed and a variable component
Exam trap — read carefully: "Variable cost" and "fixed cost" describe what happens to the total cost, but the per-unit behavior is the opposite of what the name suggests.
  • Variable cost → total changes with volume, but per-unit stays constant (e.g., $0.50/burger meat cost, always)
  • Fixed cost → total stays constant, but per-unit changes — it decreases as volume increases (e.g., $2,300 rent spread over more burgers = lower rent-per-burger)
A question that asks "which cost stays constant per unit?" is asking about variable costs — even though "fixed" sounds like the word that should mean constant.

Relevant range: the span of activity over which a cost's fixed or variable behavior can reliably be treated as a straight line. Real cost lines curve at the extremes (early units often cost more; later units benefit from economies of scale), so the relevant range is the middle band where the straight-line assumption is safe to use.

Stepped costs, per the Topic 11.8 textbook review: whether to treat a stepped cost as fixed or variable for analysis depends on the width of the steps. Wide steps (like the ice cream supervisor — one hire per 2,000 gallons) → treat as a fixed cost within the relevant range. Narrow, frequent steps → close enough to a smooth line to approximate as a variable cost.

03 · Analyzing Mixed Costs

Topic 11.3 — Scattergraph & High-Low Methods

Before a mixed cost can be used in C-V-P analysis, it must be separated into its fixed and variable components. Two methods are covered in this course:

Scattergraph Method

Plot every data point, then hand-draw a "regression line" that best fits the cloud of points. Uses all the data, but the line-drawing is subjective — two different people will get slightly different answers.

High-Low Method

Identify only the highest and lowest activity levels (and their associated costs), then compute the line between just those two points. Mathematically exact for those two points, but ignores every other data point.

REMEMBER THIS (verbatim):
  • Two common techniques for analyzing mixed costs are: Scattergraph method, High-low method
  • With both methods, where the line intercepts the cost axis represents the fixed cost, and the slope of the line represents the variable cost per unit
  • The scattergraph method involves visually fitting a straight line (the regression line) through data points plotted on a graph
  • With the high-low method, the high and the low levels of activity are used to define an estimate of the total cost line
High-Low Method Not on official formula sheet
Variable Cost per Unit = Highest Cost − Lowest CostHighest Activity − Lowest Activity
Fixed Cost = Total Cost − (Variable Cost per Unit × Activity Level)
Use either the high point or the low point in the fixed-cost formula — they'll always produce the same answer. That agreement is a built-in check on your work.
Worked example: Vantage Corp — low point 1,000 machine hours/$5,000; high point 5,000 machine hours/$13,000. Variable cost = ($13,000 − $5,000) ÷ (5,000 − 1,000) = $2.00/hour. Fixed cost = $13,000 − (5,000 × $2.00) = $3,000 (checks out from the low point too: $5,000 − (1,000 × $2.00) = $3,000). Total cost equation: $3,000 + $2.00 × machine hours.

On a scattergraph, the same logic applies visually: the y-intercept of the regression line is the fixed cost, and the slope (rise ÷ run) is the variable cost per unit.

04 · Contribution Margin & the C-V-P Equation

Topic 11.4

Contribution Margin
Contribution Margin = Sales Revenue − Variable Costs
The amount left from each sale after covering variable costs — what the business gets to keep. It first pays off fixed costs; once fixed costs are fully covered, every additional dollar of contribution margin becomes pure profit.
Contribution Margin Ratio
Contribution Margin Ratio = Contribution MarginSales Revenue
The percentage of each sales dollar kept as contribution margin. Variable Cost Ratio + Contribution Margin Ratio always = 100% — every sales dollar splits into exactly those two pieces.
The Core C-V-P Equation
Sales Revenue − Variable Costs − Fixed Costs = Target Income
At breakeven, Target Income = 0. For any other target profit, just plug in the target — same equation, no code needed. Expanded: Sales Revenue = Sales Price × Number of Units; Variable Costs = Variable Cost per Unit × Number of Units, or Variable Costs = Variable Cost Ratio × Sales Revenue.
REMEMBER THIS (verbatim):
  • Contribution Margin = Sales Revenue − Variable Costs
  • Sales Revenue − Variable Costs − Fixed Costs = Target Income
  • At breakeven, Target Income = 0
  • Sales Revenue = Sales Price × Number of Units
  • Variable Costs = Variable Cost per Unit × Number of Units
  • Variable Costs = Variable Cost Ratio × Sales Revenue
Break-Even & Target Profit Not on official formula sheet
Break-Even Units = Fixed CostsContribution Margin per Unit
Break-Even Sales $ = Fixed CostsContribution Margin Ratio
Units for Target Profit = Fixed Costs + Target ProfitCM per Unit
The official PA/OA formula sheet only shows the second half of these formulas ("Fixed Costs / " is missing at the top). Memorize the full versions — don't rely on the sheet alone for break-even.
Worked example (Jewels Baseball Gloves): Price $200/glove, variable cost $110/glove, fixed costs $63,000. Contribution margin = $90/glove. Break-even = $63,000 ÷ $90 = 700 units ($140,000 in sales). At 1,200 gloves (20% more than 1,000), profit jumps from $27,000 to $45,000 — a ~67% increase, far outpacing the 20% sales increase, because fixed costs never move while contribution margin grows proportionally with sales. (This is the mechanism behind operating leverage — see Topic 11.7.)
Rounding rule: when a target-profit or break-even calculation produces a fractional number of units, always round UP to the next whole unit — rounding down would leave the target just short.

Always verify your answer by plugging the result back into the C-V-P equation. Trust the algebra, but check it.

05 · Visualizing C-V-P With Graphs

Topic 11.5

Graphing convention (always): dollars on the Y-axis, activity level on the X-axis.

LineStarts atSlope representsHow to recognize it
Total Revenue LineOrigin ($0, 0 units)Selling price per unitOnly line that starts at zero — there's no such thing as fixed revenue
Total Cost LineFixed cost amount (y-intercept)Variable cost per unitStarts above zero, even at zero units produced
Fixed Cost LineFixed cost amount0 (flat horizontal line)Never moves, regardless of activity
Break-even point (Point G): where the Total Revenue line and Total Cost line intersect — Revenue = Total Cost, so Profit = $0. Left of that point is the loss area (cost exceeds revenue); right of it is the profit area (revenue exceeds cost). The vertical distance between the two lines at any point equals the loss or profit at that volume.
Constructing a C-V-P Graph
1. Axes → 2. Fixed cost line → 3. Total cost line → 4. Revenue line → 5. Label break-even
Draw dollars (vertical) and units (horizontal) axes. Draw the fixed cost line (horizontal). Draw the total cost line starting at the fixed cost amount, sloping up at the variable cost per unit. Draw the total revenue line starting at the origin, sloping up at the selling price per unit. Label where revenue and total cost intersect as the break-even point.

Profit Graph — an alternative view. Instead of two separate lines (revenue and cost), a profit graph plots a single line showing net profit at each activity level. Only two points are needed to draw it:

Constructing a Profit Graph
Point 1: (0 units, −Fixed Cost)   →   Point 2: (Break-even units, $0)
At zero units, there's no revenue to offset the fixed cost, so profit = a loss equal to fixed cost. At the break-even point, profit = $0 by definition. Connect those two points and extend the line. The slope of the profit line = contribution margin per unit — each additional unit sold adds exactly one unit's worth of CM straight to the bottom line.
Telling the two graph types apart at a glance: a C-V-P graph shows revenue starting at $0 and cost starting at a positive dollar value — two separate lines. A profit graph shows a single line starting BELOW zero (a negative value, equal to fixed cost) at zero units. If you see a line beginning in negative territory, you're looking at a profit graph.

06 · Effects of Sales Mix on Profitability

Topic 11.6

Sales Mix
The relative proportion of total sales dollars (or units) represented by each product
Sales mix can be expressed either in sales dollars or in units sold — a problem might hand you either one, and the same weighted-average method applies.
REMEMBER THIS (verbatim):
  • Sales mix is the proportion of sales revenue represented by each of a company's products
  • Changes in sales mix can affect profits because not all products have the same contribution margin
  • Other things being equal, to maximize profits, management should put greater emphasis on the sale of products with higher contribution margin ratios

Why this matters: once a company sells multiple products with different contribution margin ratios, a single break-even formula no longer works — the answer depends on which products the sales come from. A shift toward higher-CM-ratio products increases total profit even with zero change in total revenue.

Multi-Product Break-Even Not on official formula sheet
Break-Even Sales = Fixed CostsWeighted-Average Contribution Margin Ratio (WACMR)
Four-step method: (1) compute each product's CM ratio, (2) compute each product's sales-mix %, (3) multiply and sum → WACMR, (4) divide fixed costs by the WACMR.
Worked example (Kepler Co.): Product A — sales $20,000, CM ratio 50%; Product B — sales $30,000, CM ratio 30%; Product C — sales $10,000, CM ratio 20%. Total sales = $60,000. Sales mix: A 33.3%, B 50%, C 16.7%. WACMR = (0.333×50%) + (0.50×30%) + (0.167×20%) = 35%. With fixed costs of $17,500, break-even sales = $17,500 ÷ 0.35 = $50,000.

07 · Cost Structure & Operating Leverage

Topic 11.7

Operating Leverage
Relates to the mix of fixed vs. variable costs in a company's cost structure
Operating leverage is a measure of risk — not a measure of performance, and not related to product mix or financial (debt/equity) leverage.
Cost structureOperating leverageRisk profile
High fixed costs, low variable costsHighMore aggressive — larger profit swings in either direction as sales move
Low fixed costs, high variable costsLowMore conservative — smaller, steadier profit swings
REMEMBER THIS (verbatim):
  • Operating leverage relates to the amount of fixed costs a company has in its cost structure. High fixed costs mean high operating leverage; low fixed costs indicate low operating leverage.
  • The higher the operating leverage, the more money a company loses if sales are bad, but the more money it makes if sales are good.

Why it connects to the C-V-P equation: when sales rise, contribution margin rises proportionally with sales — but fixed costs don't move at all. Since fixed costs stay flat while contribution margin grows, every extra dollar of contribution margin beyond what's needed to cover fixed costs flows straight to profit. The higher the proportion of fixed costs, the more magnified that effect becomes — in both directions. (See the Jewels baseball glove example in Topic 04: a 20% sales increase produced a ~67% profit increase.)

Two-sided, not simply "better": high operating leverage amplifies gains above break-even, but amplifies losses below break-even by the same mechanism. A company with high fixed costs doesn't get to shrink its costs when sales slow down the way a low-leverage, mostly-variable-cost company can.

08 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The mix-ups that show up most often

  • Per-unit vs. total — the reverse trap: "constant per unit" always describes a variable cost, never a fixed one. Fixed costs are constant in total; their per-unit amount is what moves.
  • High-low method sorts by ACTIVITY, not cost: identify the highest and lowest activity level first (e.g., machine hours), then use the cost associated with each — don't sort by dollar amount.
  • Break-even formulas aren't fully on the official formula sheet: only "Fixed Costs / [blank]" appears at the bottom for both units and sales-dollar break-even. Memorize the complete formulas — Fixed Costs ÷ CM per Unit, and Fixed Costs ÷ CM Ratio.
  • Fixed costs never touch contribution margin: Contribution Margin = Sales − Variable Costs. A change in fixed costs changes profit, but has zero effect on contribution margin itself.
  • Variable Cost Ratio + Contribution Margin Ratio = 100%, always — every sales dollar splits into exactly those two pieces, with nothing left over.
  • Revenue line always starts at the origin on a C-V-P graph — there's no such thing as fixed revenue. The cost line is the one that starts above zero (at the fixed cost amount).
  • Profit graph vs. C-V-P graph: a profit graph is a single line starting below zero (negative, equal to fixed cost) at zero units — that's the tell. A C-V-P graph shows two separate lines, revenue at $0 and cost above $0.
  • Multi-product break-even needs the weighted-average CM ratio — a single-product break-even formula doesn't work once products have different contribution margin ratios.
  • Operating leverage cuts both ways — it's not simply "riskier is worse." High leverage means bigger gains above break-even AND bigger losses below it, compared to a low-leverage cost structure.
  • Stepped costs — width determines treatment: wide, infrequent steps → approximate as fixed. Narrow, frequent steps → approximate as variable.
  • Rounding rule for fractional units: always round UP on break-even or target-profit unit calculations, never down — rounding down would fall short of the goal.
