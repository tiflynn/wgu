01 · The Core Rule

Alpha = 0.05 drives every decision

Decision rule
p < 0.05 → significant → reject the null
Alpha = 0.05 means every test runs at 95% confidence. A p-value below 0.05 is significant; at or above is not significant, so you fail to reject (never "accept") the null.
  • S-S → R: Smaller than 0.05 → Significant → Reject.
  • Chi-square, t-tests and ANOVA find a significant difference. Regression finds a significant relationship. Never swap the wording.
  • Null = no significant difference (or relationship). The question is the null flipped around: write both before analysis.
  • Test statistic > critical value = significant (t uses the absolute value). It must agree with the p-value. If not, check the E-minus (E-34 is tiny) or the absolute value.

02 · Hypothesis Testing

Use a sample to judge a claim about a population

Hypothesis testing
Sample data → reject or fail to reject H₀
Results are probabilities, so we never "prove" anything. Requirements: a random sample of 30 or more.

04 · Regression

R words: relationship, predict, trend, scatter, R²

TypeVariablesBlue Owl result
Simple (OLS)1 independent (X, goes "in") predicts 1 dependent (Y).Training hours vs. order efficiency: R² = 0.339 (weak), p = 0.00073. y = 0.013x + 0.869; 10 hours → 0.999.
Multiple2+ independent, 1 dependent. Judge fit with adjusted R².Training + total experience: adj. R² = 0.39, Significance F = 0.00047, p = 0.020 and 0.044. y = 0.009T + 0.010E + 0.855 (10 h, 2 yrs → 0.965). 61% unexplained.
LogisticDependent is binary (yes/no, 0/1).Job satisfaction vs. stay/leave: coefficient 1.829, p = 0.009, odds ratio 6.228. Higher satisfaction → more likely to stay.
Time seriesTime is the independent variable; 30+ equal intervals.Patty weight by week: R² = 0.034, p = 0.329. Not significant.
  • R² = goodness of fit (0 to 1), not correlation r (−1 to 1) and not p. Report a fit word plus percent explained. A model can be significant yet weak.
  • Use the X variable's p-value (the bottom one). Observations = sample size. Report R Square, X p-value, both coefficients, Observations.
  • Coefficients fill y = mx + b: the intercept is b (listed first), the slope is m. B before M in Excel.
  • Homoscedastic = even spread (pencil). Heteroscedastic = changing spread (ice cream cone).
  • Multicollinearity: predictors correlated with each other (total experience replaced tenure). Autocorrelation: time series points influenced by the previous one.
  • Association ≠ causation. Say "explained by", never "caused by". Positive/negative = direction only.

06 · Planning & Decision Tools

Match the tool to the question

Monte Carlo simulation
Many random runs → range of outcomes
Inputs use mean and SD, min / most likely / max, or percent defective. Blue Owl: 10,000 runs, mean $4.35, SD $0.30, min $3.17, max $5.53, 95% between $3.75 and $4.95 → the $5.00 budget is very likely enough. What-if instead changes one input and watches the output.
Decision tree
EV = (favorable payoff × prob.) + (unfavorable payoff × prob.)
Payoff = profit per unit × demand. Probabilities total 100%. Pick the highest EV. Limits: raw material costs (profit/unit), layoffs (probability), diet habits (demand), plus non-financial factors.
Linear programming
Maximize Z = 60x₁ + 120x₂ + 120x₃ subject to 60x₁ + 100x₂ + 140x₃ ≤ 2,200
M words: maximize/minimize under constraints by changing the mix. Solver: 5 two-tops, 16.2 four-tops, 2 six-tops = $2,484.
Break-even
Fixed cost ÷ contribution margin = $3,800 ÷ $10 = 380
Contribution margin = $15 price − $5 cost = $10 per customer. Revenue = total cost at the break-even point.
Crossover
Cheapest option changes with volume
Vendors: $0 + $0.45, $500 + $0.38, $750 + $0.29 per transaction. The course chooses Vendor 3 at about 4,500+ monthly transactions.
Cluster analysis
Z-scores → K-means (k = 3) → segments
Most useful after data exists. Budget Regulars: 15 visits, $8.28 (loyalty/combos). Casual Diners: 8 visits, $11.70 (upsell). Convenience: 3 visits, $16.95, 75% takeout (mobile ordering).
Control chart
Average line + UCL + LCL over time
Statistical quality control. A point outside the limits signals special-cause variation. Express example: a drive-through timer with a 2-minute upper limit.

07 · Easy to Confuse & Course Quirks

Two of the course numbers do not quite add up

  • t-test vs. ANOVA: 2 groups vs. 3 or more. Chi-square vs. t/ANOVA: counts vs. means.
  • R² vs. r vs. p: fit vs. correlation vs. significance.
  • Simple vs. multiple: count the X's. Logistic: binary Y.
Crossover math: Vendors 1 and 3 actually cross near 4,688 transactions (750 ÷ 0.16), so at 4,500 Vendor 1 is about $30 cheaper. The course answer is Vendor 3. Linear programming: Solver gives 16.2 four-tops ($2,484); rounding to 16 earns $2,460. Chi-square: table p = 0.3827, narrative 0.323. 1-sample t: table mean $3,190.33, narrative $3,179. None change the conclusion; follow the course answer on the exam.

08 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The mix-ups that show up most often

  • Reject vs. fail to reject: never say "accept the null" or "prove no difference".
  • Difference vs. relationship: chi-square, t, ANOVA find differences; regression finds relationships.
  • t-test needs |t| > critical; E-08 is tiny, so p < 0.05.
  • R² is not r. Significant does not mean strong.
  • Binary outcome → logistic. More than one X → multiple regression and adjusted R².
  • Decision tree → highest EV, not highest profit per unit.
  • Break-even divides by margin, not by price.
  • ANOVA tells you something differs; post hoc tells you where.
  • Association is not causation, and positive does not mean good.
