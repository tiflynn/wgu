01 · Results-Based Management (RBM)

A cyclical life-cycle approach that learns from successes and failures

  • RBM is a management approach of continuous learning and improvement from the organization's successes and failures. It needs wide participation from all stakeholders with clearly defined accountability.
  • Widely adopted by non-profits, but also used by for-profit organizations. Best results come when goals are transparent, measurable and flexible to change.
  • There are five stages, but the process is cyclical, so it is called a life-cycle approach. The benefit is long-term strategic planning that improves and grows based on past results.

Exam keywords (Express session): life cycle, results, ongoing, partnership all signal RBM. RBM is team-based (everyone who can influence the outcome), focused on long-term impact, and uses simplified indicators. Practice question: a charity wants an ongoing monitoring strategy to confirm it achieves its goals through actual results. Answer: RBM, not Net Promoter Score, balanced scorecard or big data analysis.

02 · Index Numbers: Business Improvement Analytics

One base period, one number: above 100 is an increase, below 100 is a decrease

  • The Consumer Price Index (CPI) is published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics and measures how much households spend on baskets of goods. It is watched because it signals inflation, which affects interest rates and the prices of products.
  • The CPI is a weighted composite index: the goods in the basket are not sold at the same price or in the same quantity.
  • Index numbers can factor into short-term and long-term decisions on contracts and growth.
Simple index (one product over time)
It = (Yt ÷ Y0) × 100
Y0 = base-period value, Yt = current value. Hint: Today goes on Top, Base goes on Bottom. Index − 100 = percent change. Express traps: forgetting × 100 (a bait answer such as 9.429 or 1.25).
ExampleCalculationResult
Flour $4.00 base, $4.50 today4.50 ÷ 4.00 × 100112.5: a 12.5% increase
Gas $1 in the base year, $4 today (Express)4 ÷ 1 × 100400
Big Mac $0.49 in 1968, $4.62 in 2025 (Express)4.62 ÷ 0.49 × 100942.9, not 9.429
Simple composite index (several goods, equal amounts)
Σ current prices ÷ Σ base prices × 100
Three flour suppliers: $13.50 ÷ $12.50 × 100 = 108, an 8% increase across all suppliers combined.
Weighted composite index (diverse mix, different volumes)
Σ(qty × price) now ÷ Σ(qty × price) base × 100
Step 1: base total. Step 2: current total. Step 3: divide current by base, × 100. Whole wheat (7,500 at $4.00 → 9,000 at $4.50) and gluten-free (2,300 at $5.50 → 4,000 at $6.50): 42,650 base, 66,500 current, 66,500 ÷ 42,650 × 100 = 155.9, rounded to 156 (56% higher).
Instructor tip: the simple index is the one you really need for the OA. Caution on the weighted index: each period uses its own quantities, so 156 reflects both price and volume growth. Holding quantities fixed gives about 114. Use the course method and answer 156.

03 · Healthcare Analytics: Rate, Ratio, Proportion, Prevalence, Incidence

The CDC reports and prevents disease; these measures describe disease frequency

  • The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) is the government organization responsible for reporting and preventing disease and other risks to public health.
  • Epidemiology (CDC definition): the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to the control of health problems.
MeasureDefinitionExample
RateAn event over timeYearly flu infection, vaccination and death rates
RatioOne group compared with another group; simplify by the highest common factorGeorgia : Florida = 100,000 : 200,000 = 1 : 2
ProportionOne group compared with the whole population (cases ÷ population)Florida 200,000 ÷ 22,000,000 = 0.909%; Georgia 100,000 ÷ 10,900,000 = 0.917%
A ratio can send resources to the place with more cases, but you need each population to know whether the disease is more widespread there. In the text, Georgia has fewer cases yet a slightly higher proportion infected.
MeasureMeaningFormula or cue
PrevalenceAll existing cases in a measurement of time; the starting indicatorP = number of cases ÷ total population
IncidenceOnly new cases in a specific time. Think N: new, numberMeasured two ways (below)
Cumulative incidence (CI)Proportion of the population that develops the condition during a specified periodCI = new cases in period ÷ population at the beginning of the period
Incidence rate (IR)New cases per unit of person-time (how long each person was studied)IR = new cases in period ÷ person-time units
Express hooks: a researcher counting new cases of a virus since a date wants the incidence rate (N = new = number). "Females are twice as likely as males" compares two groups, so it is a ratio. In business terms: 14% of customers use a product = prevalence; 25 new customers a week = incidence; 10% are repeat buyers = proportion.
Two framings of cumulative incidence: the text defines it as new cases in a period over the starting population. The instructor describes it as projecting the weekly rate forward (2% × 10 weeks = 20%). Follow the keywords: new cases = incidence rate; over the next X weeks or a forecast = cumulative incidence.

04 · Education Analytics: Percentiles, Scores and Item Response

Where a score sits, what it really measures, and whether the test question works

  • Percentile: the share of the population that falls below a score. It ranks how many others you scored above, and eligibility can depend on it.
  • Standard score (z-score): how many standard deviations a score is from the mean. Unitless, so it lets you compare the same person on different scales.
z-score
z = (individual score − mean) ÷ standard deviation
Exam 81, mean 72, SD 3: z = +3. Essay 3, mean 5.5, SD 1.2: z = −2.08. Above peers on the exam but below the mean on the essay. In this course the SD is always given.
Express example: score 70, mean 50, SD 10 gives z = +2. About 95% lie within 2 SD, so the two tails hold 5% (2.5% each). You beat 97.5%, and 2.5% scored higher. Read the question mark first: "higher than you" is the right tail only.
Norm-referencedCriterion-referenced
Measures againstOther people in the same subject areaA pre-determined score
ThinkGrading on a curve; top candidates chosen when openings are limitedPass if you show competence; everyone can pass or fail
ExamplesStandard score; scholarship or debate teamDriver's written test; WGU objective exams (your OA)
  • Observed score: what you score on a single test. True score: what you would score on average if you took the test an infinite number of times under the same conditions.
  • True Score Theory: observed score = true score + random error (when there is no systematic error). No real test is free of random error.
  • Random error (flu on test day) minimizes as the sample grows. Systematic error (a typo that flips a plus to a minus) continues until its cause is eliminated.
OA strategy from the Express session: on a fill-in-the-blank like "for a test without ___ error, the ___ score is the ___ score plus the ___ error," first discard the two answers that say a test can lack random error. What remains: no systematic error, observed = true + random error.
Item analysis: item difficulty (true score, p0)
observed = [1 + p0 × (k − 1)] ÷ k
k = number of answer choices. Observed 70% with 4 choices: 2.8 = 1 + 3·p0, so 1.8 = 3·p0 and p0 = 0.60. Steps: multiply by the denominator, subtract 1, divide by (k − 1), read p0. The observed score is higher because some students guess correctly.
  • Item analysis checks whether a question truly discriminates between students who know the answer and those who are only guessing.
  • Compare high-scoring students who got it right with low-scoring students who got it right. A difference of 40% or higher means the item measures knowledge accurately.

Item response theory (IRT): reliability models

MethodHow it works
Parallel formsTwo separate tests on the same subject; participants take both and the results are compared
Test-retestThe same test is given to the same sample twice and the scores are compared
Split-halfOne test is split into two equal parts measuring the same construct; the sample takes each half and the scores are compared
A person often takes a test only once (an observed score), so a reliability index measures how an observed score compares with a true score. The text cuts these three method cards off mid-sentence; the endings above follow the standard definitions.

05 · Public Sector Analytics: Cost-Benefit, Payback and Sustainability

No revenue means measuring benefits instead of profit

  • Private companies measure payoff with profit. The public sector is funded from a tax-based budget, so performance is measured through benefits: safety, welfare and cost avoidance.
  • Decision-makers distribute the budget to programs and projects based on needs, potential benefits and cost prevention.
ConceptWhat it means in the public sector
Cost-benefit analysisDone without generating revenue or profit, so a break-even analysis of paid users cannot be performed. A new asset can be justified by the maintenance cost of the old one, reliable travel, safety and reduced emissions.
Payback periodThe time it takes to recover the initial investment, estimated once the total investment is known. With no revenue, it is measured with savings or cost avoidance (lower maintenance, monetized safety risk, and reduced emissions for an eco-friendly option).
SustainabilityThe Council on Environmental Quality was directed by executive order to leverage purchasing power for the Federal Sustainability Plan: reduce emissions of 300,000+ buildings and 600,000+ vehicles by 65% by 2030, and reach net-zero by 2050, with investment in renewable energy and conservation of fossil fuels while balancing economic growth in clean energy.
Payback period (standard simple form)
payback = initial investment ÷ annual savings
$120,000 invested, $30,000 saved per year: 4 years. The course text defines payback but gives no formula; this is the usual simple version, with the answer in units of time.
