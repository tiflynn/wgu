01 · Why Quantitative Analysis?

Decisions should rest on relevant, reliable data, not intuition

Purpose of analytics
Data → Information → Fact-based decisions → Less risk
Analytics transforms data into information that supports fact-based decisions and helps reduce risk. Everyday examples: choosing a home by school system, crime rating, and price-per-square-foot, or choosing a school by tuition, transferable credits, and graduation rates.
Quality data
Accurate + Reliable + Relevant
Collected, stored, and analyzed in a systematic and consistent way: verify the sources, check for errors or inconsistencies, and keep it up to date.
SettingDecision-Making Tools
BusinessCost-benefit analysis, scenario planning (investments, market opportunities)
HealthcareDecision trees, predictive analytics (diagnose disease, recommend treatment)
GovernmentPolicy modeling, impact assessments (effects of policies on society)
Choosing a tool: tools range from simple frameworks (SWOT analysis) to complex algorithms and simulations. Pick based on the context and goals of the decision, the complexity of the problem, data availability, and the level of uncertainty.

02 · Big Data & Data Mining

Too big for Excel

Big data
Extremely large amounts of structured and unstructured data, too large for traditional tools to store or process. Generated by companies like Amazon and Facebook.
  • Sources: social media, mobile apps and GPS, healthcare records and wearables, streaming services, scientific research, website clicks and search activity.
  • Storage: a big data warehouse on multiple servers, usually hosted in the cloud by a third-party provider that is responsible for securing it.
  • Why collect it: to understand buying behavior, attract and target customers, and innovate.
  • Data mining: the process of discovering patterns in large data sets.
Size test: the FBI's nationwide criminal database is big data. A sewing shop's year of customer addresses (one Excel file) is not.

04 · Data Quality & Errors

Reliable, valid, and clean

R

Reliable

Consistent and repeatable results. A scale shows the same weight three times in a row.

V

Valid

Measures what you intend to measure. A puzzle does not measure IQ, and running speed does not measure health.

Good data must be both. Data can be reliable without being valid.
ErrorDefinitionExample
OmissionA field is blank or incompleteA car priced at $0 because the price was never entered
Out-of-rangeA value falls outside the acceptable rangeBirth year 2013 in a study of people born 2015-2020
Inconsistent codingSame thing entered different waysGRY vs. Gray
  • Data cleaning: sort the data to expose blanks and extreme values.
  • Systematic error: consistent, repeatable, will not fix itself. Someone must intervene (recalibrate a sensor, enter the missing digit).
  • Random error: temporary, intermittent, self-correcting (static on a call, a restart after a power loss).
  • Classify by the effect, not the cause: a missing middle name is an omission error (how it happened), but it causes a systematic error.
  • Distorted: the course case study calls a dataset with an omission error distorted.
  • A data quality team with clearly defined roles speeds cleaning and supports reliable analysis.

05 · Research Design & Blinding

Is a treatment applied?

ObservationalExperimental
TreatmentNoneResearcher applies it
Control groupNoneUsually yes (often a placebo)
What you doObserve and recordApply treatment, measure response
ExamplesCohort study, quasi-experimental designControlled experiment, A/B test with random assignment
  • Cohort study: observes a specific group with shared characteristics over time. Observing and classifying only, never intervening.
  • Quasi-experimental: compares two groups under different conditions without randomly assigned treatment and control groups. Still observational.
  • Self-selected groups (people who chose remote work, or chose to join a rewards program) are observational.
  • Three elements of an experiment: Unit (who is studied), Treatment (what is applied), Response (what is measured).
BlindingWho doesn't know their group
BlindParticipants (the unit)
Double-blindParticipants + the person administering the treatment
Triple-blindParticipants + administrator + the person collecting or analyzing responses
Placebo: a look-alike with no active treatment, so participants can't tell which group they're in. Count the blindfolds to find the level.

06 · Bias & Sampling

Where research goes wrong

BiasCauseExample
Measurement biasSample is too small, not representative, or not randomSurveying the last 30 buyers
Information biasParticipants' answers aren't truthful (response or conscious bias)See below
Response biasWho is askingA boss asks about overtime; a teacher asks if the assignment helped
Conscious biasHow the question is worded (persuasive or offers a benefit)"Don't you agree this product is better?"
Faulty operationalizationSampling, questions, coding, or interpretation not defined in advance'Happy' vs. 'disgruntled' never defined
  • Good sample: random (equal chance), representative (a mini-me of the population), and large enough.
  • No magic sample size. The square root of the population is only a starting point, and "30" depends on the test.
  • Reduce response bias: anonymous surveys or a third party.
  • Reduce conscious bias: neutral wording, no benefits for particular answers.
  • Decide in advance how to handle unexpected answers (such as "maybe" to a yes/no question).

07 · Association vs. Causation

Related does not mean caused

The rule
Statistics show relationships. Only controlled experiments support causation.
A significant result does not prove one variable causes another. Other variables (limitations, hidden factors) must be evaluated first.
ToolWhat it doesExample
t-testCompares the averages of two groups; tests whether the difference is statistically significantWebinar vs. asynchronous test scores
R² (regression)Goodness of fit of a trend line, from 0 (weak) to 1 (strong)R² = 0.7238 means about 72% fit
  • Say: associated with, has a relationship with. Avoid: causes, reduces, increases, impacts.
  • Positive = trends up left to right. Negative = trends down. Neither means good or bad.
  • Hidden factor example: ice cream sales and pool visits both rise because it's hot.

08 · Types of Analytics & the Decision Process

Good, better, best

TypeQuestionLooks atExample
DescriptiveWhat happened?Past and present (lowest form)Car prices rose 2% last year
PredictiveWhat will happen?Past data to forecast; no optimizingPrices expected to rise 1.5% next year
PrescriptiveWhat should we do?Prediction + optimization (highest form)Run an ad campaign to lift a $2,000 month by 5%
Two-question test: Am I predicting? No → descriptive. Yes → Am I optimizing? Yes → prescriptive, no → predictive. Diagnostic is not one of the three.

Quantitative analysis approach:

1 · Define the problem
→
2 · Model + accurate data
→
3 · Develop & test solution
→
4 · Analyze, communicate, implement
  • Defining the problem is the most important and often hardest step. Go past symptoms to the true cause and review previous findings.
  • A good model is solvable, realistic, easy to understand and modify, with attainable input data. Even a perfect model fails on bad data.
  • Test both the model and the input data for accuracy and completeness.
  • After implementing, monitor closely. A changing economy or demand may require changes.
  • Data-driven decision process: frame the problem (scope, who to collaborate with, research question), solve it, communicate results.

09 · Exam-Trap Cheat Sheet

The mix-ups that show up most often

  • Interval vs. ratio: does zero mean none? Calendar years and temperature are interval. Age, price, revenue, and miles per gallon are ratio.
  • Satisfaction ratings are ordinal, even when shown as numbers 1-5.
  • Reliable vs. valid: same every time vs. the right thing measured.
  • Response vs. conscious bias: who is asking vs. how it's worded.
  • Measurement vs. information bias: the sample vs. the truthfulness of answers.
  • Observational vs. experimental: was a treatment applied? Voluntary or self-selected groups are observational.
  • Systematic vs. random error: judge by the effect. Systematic needs a fix; random fixes itself.
  • Omission error → distorted dataset. A blank field is an omission error whose effect is systematic.
  • Significant ≠ causes. A t-test or R² shows a relationship, never cause and effect.
  • "Last 30" is a convenience sample. The bigger problem is that it isn't random, not the number 30.
