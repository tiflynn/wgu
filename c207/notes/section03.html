01 · Probability: Measuring Risk

Risk, probability and the single-event rules

Why statistics is a managerial tool
Lower risk → higher chance of success → profit
Risk = things did not go as planned (or not as well as planned). Success = the desired outcome. Probability helps you manage both: know the options, the comparative risk of each, the uncertainties and the outcomes you want. Managing risk costs money, time and energy, but done well it raises profit and efficiency.
Probability of an event
P(E) = desired outcomes ÷ all possible outcomes
Example: 2 of 5 associates can still give a discount, so P = 2/5 = 40%.
Complement
P(not E) = 1 − P(E)
With only two outcomes (happens or not), the two probabilities always total 1 (or 100%). Given 0.35 for success, failure is 0.65. Questions often give only one side.
  • Mutually exclusive: events that cannot both happen. The intersection is 0, so the union is just the sum.
  • Without replacement: when an item is taken and not returned, the denominator changes (and the numerator too if it was a "success"). 20 tickets with 5 winners: after one winner is drawn, P(winner) = 4/19.

02 · Combining Probabilities

Intersection, union and Bayes: read the keywords

RuleCue wordsWhat you doExample
Intersection (∩)"and", "both", "all", "every"Multiply: P(A) × P(B)0.60 × 0.30 = 0.18
Union (∪)"or", "either", "any"Add, then subtract the overlap: P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)0.60 + 0.30 − 0.18 = 0.72
Bayes / conditional"given", "knowing", "if this happens, what is the chance that…"One event happens first; divide by its probability0.12 ÷ 0.40 = 0.30
Union trap: a union means "A or B, but not counted twice". The question never says so. If one answer is the plain sum (0.90), it is the wrong one. A union is always at least as large as the intersection.
Bayes (conditional) formula
P(B | A) = P(A and B) ÷ P(A)
Read "the probability of B given A". The given event is the trigger, so there is an order. That order is the difference between Bayes and an intersection. If P(B | A) = P(B), A tells you nothing about B (independent events). Think of it like the decision-tree work in Section 2: combine branch probabilities, then compare outcomes.

Probability tree: multiply along a path; all paths total 1.00

  • Permutations: order matters (first place vs. second). Combinations: order does not matter (who is on the team). Both return a whole-number count. Choosing 2 of 5: 20 permutations, 10 combinations.
  • Formulas (not emphasized): nPr = n! ÷ (n − r)! and nCr = n! ÷ (r! × (n − r)!). The instructor said combinations are not a focus of the objective assessment.

03 · Measures of Central Tendency

Mean, median and mode

MeasureHow to find itCue / useStrength or weakness
MeanAdd all values, divide by the count"Average"Pulled by outliers; never use it alone, pair it with a measure of spread
MedianSort low to high and take the middle. Even count: average the two middle values"Middle"; half the data is below and half aboveRobust to outliers; says nothing about spread
ModeThe value that occurs most often (none, one or several)The word "typical"Robust to outliers; shows frequency
  • Median position: 9 values → the 5th; 11 values → the 6th; 10 values → average of the 5th and 6th.
  • Sales example (7 reps): $1,000, $4,500, $4,600, $4,900, $5,200, $5,200, $7,500. Sum = $32,900, so mean = $4,700; median = $4,900; mode = $5,200. The two outliers (Juan and Jacqueline) are why the mean misleads.
Takeaway: the median is the most robust center, the mode shows what happens most, and the mean needs a measure of spread beside it (usually the standard deviation).

04 · Measures of Spread

Range, IQR, variance and standard deviation

  • Range = highest − lowest. Sales example: $7,500 − $1,000 = $6,500. Uses only the extremes.
  • Interquartile range (IQR) = Q3 − Q1. Q1 is the median of the lower half ($4,500) and Q3 the median of the upper half ($5,200), so IQR = $700. Leaves out the extremes.
  • Variance and standard deviation (SD) both measure how spread out the data is. The course uses them almost interchangeably when asked "which is a measure of spread?" The SD is the square root of the variance, so it is in the original units.

05 · Box Plots and Skew

A communication tool built from five numbers

1 · Sort
→
2 · Find the median
→
3 · Find Q1 and Q3
→
4 · Draw the box (Q1 to Q3)
→
5 · Whiskers to min and max
  • The box width is the IQR; the whiskers span the range (this course ignores outliers on box plots).
  • Sales example: min $1,000, Q1 $4,500, median $4,900, Q3 $5,200, max $7,500.
  • You will not draw a box plot on the objective assessment, but you may need to read the range, median, quartiles and IQR, and spot skew. Managers read box plots fast, once someone explains how.
Skew rule: the long whisker points toward the skew. A well-controlled process is symmetric; a shift in one hour shows up as skew.

06 · The Normal Distribution

Empirical Rule, slices and what the mean and SD do

Conditions for the Empirical Rule: mean ≈ median ≈ mode; sample size greater than 30; about 50% of the data on each side of the mean.
Memorize
±1 SD ≈ 68% · ±2 SD ≈ 95% · ±3 SD ≈ 99.7%
Range = mean ± (number of SDs × SD). Mean 60 and SD 5: 55–65 (68%), 50–70 (95%), 45–75 (99.7%). The text uses 68.3 / 95.4 / 99.7 and the lecture 68.2 / 95.4 / 99.7; they are the same rule, so choose the closest answer.
SliceSizeHow to get it
Mean to ±1 SD34.1% each side68.2% ÷ 2
±1 SD to ±2 SD13.6% each side(95.4 − 68.2) ÷ 2
±2 SD to ±3 SD2.1% each side(99.7 − 95.4) ÷ 2
Beyond ±3 SD0.15% each side(100 − 99.7) ÷ 2

Draw it out (bring a whiteboard and a dry-erase marker)

1 · Sketch the curve, mark the mean
→
2 · Mark ±1, ±2, ±3 SD
→
3 · Label the slices
→
4 · Shade the region
→
5 · Add the slices
  • Example: mean 50, SD 4, between 46 and 58 → 34.1 + 34.1 + 13.6 = 81.8%.
  • One tail: above mean + 1 SD = (100 − 68.2) ÷ 2 = 15.9%. Above mean − 1 SD = 34.1 + 50 = 84.1%.
  • Lecture rounding: the lecture quoted about 97.5%, 83.9% and 15.7% for its practice problems; slice sums give 97.7%, 84.1% and 15.9%. Expect answer choices close to these, not exact.
  • IQ example (mean 100, SD 15): 70–130 = ±2 SD = 95.4%; 85–115 = ±1 SD = 68.3%; above 115 = 15.87%.
Qualitative questions: a change in the mean slides the whole curve left or right. A change in the SD makes it wider and flatter (larger) or narrower and taller (smaller).

07 · Z-Scores

How many standard deviations from the mean

Formula (memorize)
z = (x − mean) ÷ SD
Subtract first, then divide. The size counts the SDs; the sign gives the side: positive = above the mean, negative = below, 0 = exactly at the mean. For a population: z = (x − μ) ÷ σ.
SituationCalculationMeaning
Typing 50 wpm; peers average 45, SD 2(50 − 45) ÷ 2 = +2.52.5 SDs above peers
Typing 40 wpm; peers average 42, SD 2(40 − 42) ÷ 2 = −11 SD below peers
Processed 58; average 68, SD 11(58 − 68) ÷ 11 = −0.91About 1 SD below the mean
Why it matters: z-scores put values from different samples on one scale, so you can compare and benchmark. A negative z-score is not automatically "bad"; it only means below the mean. You may have to calculate a z-score or place a point on a distribution, but you will not draw a graph for it.

08 · Easy to Confuse and Exam Tips

The mix-ups that show up most often

Mix-upHow to tell them apart
Intersection vs. union"and / both / all / every" multiply. "or / either" add and subtract the overlap, so the union is larger.
Intersection vs. BayesBayes has an order ("given", "knowing", "if"). An intersection has no trigger.
Median vs. meanThe median counts positions (half above, half below); the mean uses size, so outliers move it.
Mode wording"Typical" or "most common" means mode.
Range vs. IQRRange uses the extremes; IQR (Q3 − Q1) is the box.
Variance vs. SDBoth measure spread. SD is the square root of variance, in the original units.
Z-score signNegative means below the mean, not "bad". The size tells how many SDs.
68-95-99.7 vs. "less than"The rule gives ± bands. For one-sided questions, add the slices.
Permutation vs. combinationOrder matters = permutation. Order does not matter = combination.
  • Objective assessment: part vocabulary, part math. Questions can include numbers you do not need. Expect normal-distribution problems. You will not run a regression in Excel.
  • Bring: a whiteboard and a dry-erase marker (not permanent).
